Run a two-year **spinout-friction and outside-capital pilot**, and decline the compulsory course, pitch contest, and branded incubator.

The working hypothesis is that eighteen-month IP approval and weak investor links—not a shortage of entrepreneurship theater—are the limiting constraints. In the first 90 days, the provost should name the vice president for research as owner and establish a dated baseline: disclosures by department, time at each approval step, founder drop-off and departure reasons, company formation, first financing, survival, and investor response time. Compare with the university's prior three years, matched universities, and a staged departmental rollout so the process change has a useful comparison.

Allocate the $4 million as follows: $2.2 million for a delegated IP fast lane with standard terms, published service levels, and overflow legal capacity; $1.0 million for recurring access to specialist outside investors and operators through open office hours, diligence support, and visits; $500,000 for independent measurement; and a $300,000 reserve. This is general institutional-friction and outside-link work, not a hub, subsidy, or eligibility-limited cohort, so cohort-selection and community-density gates are inapplicable. The university can control its approval process and access program, but should not claim it controls regional startup outcomes.

Pre-register separate rules. **Stop at month 6** if the owner lacks authority to change IP terms or obtain reliable funnel data. **Change at month 9** if median approval time is not below nine months, by simplifying delegation and outsourcing overflow rather than adding events. **Expand at month 12** only if approval time is below six months and the staged comparison shows materially less founder drop-off; use the reserve to extend the proven workflow, not to build space. **Remeasure at month 24** company formation, financing, two-year survival where observable, investor relationships, and founder/company flows before renewal.

Do not impose local-residency or local-investor rules. Treat founders who leave, alumni who return, and outside capital relationships as distinct flows; durable companies and networks matter more than keeping every person inside the city boundary.
