# Raw evaluation output

Case ID: scenario-01

Reviewer ID: growth-baseline-001

The user-learning owner should own the question, the sample, the interview or observation protocol, and the interpretation of evidence. The growth lead can own recruitment execution, but only as a service to that learning plan. This distinction matters because a channel's ability to generate clicks or signups says little about whether clinic managers have an important inventory problem.

Before outreach, the user-learning owner should define the 20-clinic sample by relevant dimensions: independent versus small group, clinic size, purchasing role, current inventory system, ordering volume, and recent stockout experience. Keep a meaningful cold-prospect stratum and do not replace hard-to-recruit segments with whoever responds fastest. Use a neutral invitation to a paid workflow-research session, not product claims or a landing page that selects for enthusiasm. Compensation should be a fixed research incentive, unrelated to product interest.

Growth can prepare contact lists, run several outreach methods, schedule sessions, and report recruitment progress. It may improve subject lines, timing, and follow-up enough to fill the predetermined cells, but it must preserve the invitation, screening rules, and sample mix. Recruitment metrics belong in an operations report: eligible contacts reached, response rate, qualification rate, scheduled sessions, completed sessions, cost per completed session, and representation by sample cell. Ad click-through, email opens, and landing-page signups are diagnostic for recruiting mechanics only. They are neither opportunity scores nor reasons to change the research question.

The first sessions should examine existing behavior without showing a prototype. Ask each manager to demonstrate a recent ordering cycle and a recent stockout or near-stockout using real artifacts where permission allows: spreadsheets, practice-management screens, supplier portals, invoices, messages, and reorder lists. Record who notices the need, how a purchase is approved, what information is missing, how long the work takes, what errors occur, what stockouts cost, which workarounds exist, and whether the manager has tried to solve the problem already. Separate reported frustration from observed behavior and quantify frequency and consequence rather than relying on adjectives such as "painful."

After an initial tranche, the user-learning owner should compare cases and identify a narrow repeated job and the conditions under which it matters. Follow up on contradictions instead of averaging them away. Only then should the team test a lightweight prototype or concierge version against the observed workflow. The prototype test should ask managers to use it on a real or faithfully reconstructed task, reveal what access and integration it would require, and make a concrete next-step commitment such as supplying data for a pilot, scheduling a live trial, or discussing budget. Compliments and hypothetical willingness are weak evidence.

The decision review should keep two scorecards separate. The recruitment scorecard asks whether the intended sample was reached efficiently. The demand-evidence scorecard asks whether a sufficiently coherent segment repeatedly exhibits the problem, incurs meaningful cost or risk, uses inadequate alternatives, can adopt the proposed intervention, and makes costly commitments to continue. The user-learning owner makes the demand recommendation from the second scorecard. Growth reports what it learned about reaching the segment but does not declare a winning acquisition channel until the team has evidence of a product worth acquiring users for.
