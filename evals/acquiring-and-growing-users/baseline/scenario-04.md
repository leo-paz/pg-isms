# Raw evaluation output

Case ID: scenario-04

Reviewer ID: growth-baseline-001

The primary workflow is product discovery and problem validation, not acquisition. The current evidence mostly measures attention under unusually favorable conditions: a giveaway produced email addresses, and a webinar produced three enthusiastic statements. None of it establishes that procurement teams repeatedly encounter a valuable problem, can give the assistant the required workflow access, will use it in real work, can clear security and deployment barriers, or will pay.

The founders should pause work on a referral loop and a scale-oriented activation dashboard. Those systems would optimize movement through a funnel whose meaningful stages are not yet known. A waitlist conversion rate or referral rate could grow while the company learns nothing about adoption in procurement environments.

For the next stage, a product or user-learning owner should recruit a deliberately varied set of procurement practitioners, including cold participants who were not attracted by the giveaway or webinar. Observe live or recently completed procurement work with appropriate permission. Examine actual requests, supplier comparisons, approvals, policies, systems, handoffs, exceptions, and audit requirements. Identify a narrow job in which the cost of delay, error, or manual effort is both frequent and consequential. Document who feels the problem, who controls data and integrations, who approves deployment, and who holds budget; those may be different people.

After repeated evidence identifies a narrow workflow, test the smallest service or prototype that can participate in real work. Ask design partners for increasingly costly commitments: access to representative data, time from the workflow owner, security or legal review, a scheduled live pilot, agreed success measures, and eventually payment or a credible procurement step. Observe use rather than treating praise, feature requests, or hypothetical purchase intent as adoption. A pilot is informative only if it encounters the real permissions, handoffs, and failure conditions the production product would face.

Acquiring-and-growing-users has a limited supporting role. It can help find representative research participants, maintain contact records, schedule sessions, and compare the practicality of reaching clearly defined segments. Outreach performance should be labeled as recruitment operations, not demand evidence. Growth may also help instrument a small learning funnel once a live pilot exists, but the stages should represent substantive progress: qualified workflow owner, observed problem, access approved, pilot started, core task completed, repeated use, and commercial commitment. That is not yet a scale dashboard.

The handoff to meaningful acquisition work should occur only after several independent organizations in a coherent segment complete the core job, repeat it, clear the necessary deployment constraints, and make commercial commitments. At that point, the team can test a repeatable message and channel against qualified buyers. A referral mechanism should come later still, after users have obtained enough dependable value that recommending the product is a natural behavior rather than an incentive layered over unproven demand.
