# 60-day plan

The constraint is not lead volume; it is getting a dental group through integration and into a durable scheduling habit. The startup should not commit to the reseller launch or hire three salespeople yet. With a six-to-eight-week procurement and integration cycle, a campaign next month would create demand the team cannot reliably activate, and demo optimization would improve the wrong part of the funnel.

For the next 60 days, make one founder the directly responsible owner of the full revenue funnel and give a product/engineering lead ownership of activation. Use the six pilots as the working cohort, with each account tracked from invitation through week-eight use. Keep the reseller opportunity alive only as a small, reversible validation—not a broad launch commitment—until price, economics, lead ownership, support obligations, and activation capacity are explicit.

## Outcomes and guardrails

By day 60, aim to:

- Raise invited-manager setup starts from 70% to at least 85%.
- Raise completed practice-management integrations from 35% to at least 65%.
- Raise week-eight scheduling use from 22% to at least 45%, measured by meaningful weekly scheduling activity rather than login.
- Get at least four of the six groups to a defined recurring-use milestone, or learn precisely which segment and workflow can do so.
- Validate a repeatable acquisition message through 20–30 narrowly targeted conversations and 5–8 qualified next steps, without adding full-time sales headcount.
- Reach a written reseller term sheet and run a small lead test only if activation milestones are on track.

Do not count reseller introductions, trade-show badge scans, demos, or signed pilots as success. The operating metric is the number of groups that complete integration and sustain the core scheduling workflow.

## Days 1–10: diagnose and take ownership

### Acquisition

- Pause broad campaigns and sales hiring. The CEO continues founder-led selling, but targets groups resembling the two retained customers: same practice-management system, number of sites, scheduling pain, implementation owner, and urgency.
- Interview the two retained groups, the four stalled groups, and at least two relevant people per group: the economic buyer and the office-manager/operator. Reconstruct why they bought, where setup stalled, the time to first scheduled shift, and what made the product become—or fail to become—a habit.
- Ask the retained groups for introductions only after documenting the attributes that make them a good fit. Referrals are evidence of value, not yet evidence that the company can onboard a broad market.

### Activation

- Instrument one account-level funnel: invite received → setup started → integration credentials supplied → integration connected → data verified → first schedule published → first repeated scheduling week. Report conversion and elapsed time at every step, segmented by practice-management system and group.
- Run five observed setup sessions. Do not rely only on surveys: watch managers attempt setup, record confusing language, missing permissions, handoffs, and delays.
- Assign every pilot a named implementation owner on both sides, a kickoff date, a shared checklist, and a target date for first value. Contact the four stalled groups immediately with a recovery plan rather than another demo.

### Retention

- Define the core retained behavior with the two successful groups—for example, publishing or filling a minimum number of shifts in each of four consecutive weeks. Track this weekly by group and site.
- Establish short weekly operational calls for all active pilots until integration and first value are complete. Capture product issues separately from customer-owned procurement or credential delays.

### Ownership

- CEO/founder: acquisition learning, segment choice, and reseller negotiation.
- Product/engineering lead: integration completion, setup redesign, and time to first value.
- Customer-success founder or strongest operator: pilot recovery and recurring-use playbook.
- One shared dashboard is reviewed twice weekly. Every stalled account has a cause, next action, owner, and due date. No metric has two owners.

## Days 11–30: repair activation and test acquisition manually

### Acquisition

- Turn the retained-customer interviews into a narrow ideal customer profile and problem-led message. Run founder outreach to 20–30 matched dental groups through referrals and direct contact. The call to action is an implementation-readiness assessment, not a generic demo.
- Qualify for the practice-management system, authority to grant integration access, a named operational champion, an agreed scheduling use case, and a six-to-eight-week implementation window. Decline or defer poor-fit pilots.
- Test two messages and track reply → discovery → qualified implementation plan, plus source and segment. Keep volume low enough that every new group can receive the improved onboarding.

### Activation

- Fix the highest-frequency blockers found in observation. Likely interventions include a shorter setup path, plain-language permission instructions, preflight compatibility checks, an integration status screen, saved progress, and clear escalation when credentials or vendor approval are missing.
- Separate work that truly requires engineering from a concierge workaround. For the current pilots, provide scheduled integration sessions and proactive status updates; meanwhile, build only fixes that remove a repeated bottleneck.
- Require a mutual implementation plan before any new pilot starts. The plan names required credentials, customer and startup owners, dates, dependencies, success criteria, and the first live scheduling event.
- Set service-level targets: setup begins within two business days of kickoff, blockers are acknowledged within one business day, and a connected account reaches first value within five business days after credentials and approvals are available.

### Retention

- Build a 30/45/60-day adoption cadence around the first recurring workflow. Show each group its usage and outcome, train a backup manager, and agree on the next set of sites only after the first site is active.
- For the two retained groups, identify the product behavior and operational result they would miss. Ask what would cause them to expand or churn. For the four others, distinguish recoverable implementation failures from weak demand; stop investing in accounts that complete integration but still lack a recurring use case.

### Reseller decision gate at day 30

Negotiate now, but do not promise a trade-show launch. Require a written proposal covering price, revenue share, exclusivity, who owns the customer and data, qualification criteria, implementation and support duties, lead handoff, renewal, termination, and marketing claims. Offer a controlled test of 10–15 reseller-sourced introductions with no exclusivity or minimum volume.

Proceed with even that test only if recent cohorts show at least 55% integration completion, median time from available credentials to connection is declining, and the team has capacity for every accepted account. Otherwise, defer the launch by 30–60 days and use the reseller's willingness to wait as a test of partnership quality.

## Days 31–60: prove repeatability and make the scale decision

### Acquisition

- Continue the best-performing founder-led message and segment. Document a lightweight sales playbook: qualification questions, disqualifiers, proof points, implementation requirements, expected timeline, and a handoff checklist.
- If the reseller passes the day-30 gate, give it the same qualification rubric and run the limited lead batch. Compare reseller leads with founder/referral leads on qualified rate, integration start, implementation burden, and expected economics—not raw lead count.
- Do not optimize a polished demo until discovery shows that the demo itself is a material loss point. Tailor the demo to the core scheduling job and make implementation requirements visible before commitment.

### Activation and retention

- Ship the second round of setup fixes based on funnel data, then retest with people who did not see the previous flow.
- Hold cohort reviews at days 45 and 60. For each group, inspect time between stages, first-value attainment, weekly core behavior, support load, and stated expansion or renewal intent.
- Turn successful onboarding into a checklist that another team member can execute. Run at least two new accounts through it with the founder observing but not leading; this tests whether the process, rather than founder heroics, works.
- Seek a concrete commitment from retained groups: paid conversion, expansion to another site, or a dated procurement step. Praise and referrals are encouraging, but willingness to pay or expand is the stronger retention signal.

## Day-60 decisions

Scale acquisition only if one segment shows a repeatable path: at least 65% of qualified groups complete integration, at least 45% sustain the core week-eight behavior, time to first value is predictable, and support effort per launch is falling. If those conditions hold, hire one implementation/customer-success owner before—or alongside—one salesperson, then add sales capacity one person at a time against demonstrated pipeline and onboarding capacity.

Accept a broader reseller campaign only if the pilot economics remain attractive after revenue share and support cost, reseller leads meet the qualification bar, and ownership terms preserve the startup's access to customer learning. If the thresholds are missed, keep the team small, narrow the segment further, and spend the next cycle fixing the dominant activation or retention failure. The company earns distribution by proving it can turn qualified demand into retained use.
