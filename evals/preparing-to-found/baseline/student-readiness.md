# Student Readiness

Case ID: student-readiness

Reviewer ID: preparing-baseline-001

## Frozen response

Use the year to become more capable and to encounter real problems, not to imitate a startup. A good outcome is not necessarily “I have a company in twelve months.” It is “I can build useful things quickly, I know several people I would trust under stress, and I have first-hand knowledge of a problem that somebody cares enough to solve.”

I would structure the year around three tracks:

1. **Get unusually good at making things.** Pick projects whose usefulness can be tested by people outside your friend group. Ship small versions in weeks, watch people use them, maintain the ones that earn repeat use, and stop the ones that do not. The goal is a tight loop of building, showing, listening, and improving—not a polished pitch deck or a contrived “startup” brand.
2. **Work closely with strong people.** Take hard classes, join a serious engineering or research team, contribute to an open-source project, or work at a small company where you can see product and customer decisions. Pay attention to who is energetic, honest, technically effective, and calm when work goes wrong. Future cofounders are usually discovered through substantive shared work, not networking events.
3. **Develop problem taste.** Spend time with users in a few domains you can genuinely access: a campus lab, a student organization, a local business, a previous employer, or a technical community. Ask how work actually happens, where time or money is lost, and what people have already tried. Keep a problem log, but do not force every annoyance into a company idea.

A practical twelve-month cadence could be:

- **Months 1–2:** Choose one technically stretching project with reachable users. Interview 10–15 of them before deciding the first narrow use case. Establish a weekly shipping rhythm.
- **Months 3–5:** Put the project in users’ hands. Measure repeat use or another concrete behavior. Ask for a small payment if it solves a real problem; payment is a better signal than compliments. Work with one or two peers and notice how collaboration feels.
- **Months 6–8:** Do a second project or join a demanding small team, preferably in a different domain. Learn one neglected founder skill such as customer discovery, writing, recruiting, or basic sales. Do it through real work rather than courses alone.
- **Months 9–10:** Review the evidence. Which problems kept recurring? Who repeatedly showed up and delivered? Did anyone pull a product out of you by using, requesting, or paying for it?
- **Months 11–12:** If there is strong pull—a trusted collaborator, a problem you understand, and users making real commitments—run a focused startup experiment. If there is not, choose the next environment that maximizes learning and proximity to good people, such as a strong early-stage company or research group. Do not manufacture urgency just to meet a deadline.

Keep your personal options healthy: finish the degree unless a genuinely exceptional opportunity makes the tradeoff clear, avoid lifestyle inflation, and learn enough about employment agreements and intellectual property to keep side projects clean. Spend very little on logos, incorporation, conferences, “founder” content, or networking without a concrete purpose.

At the end of each month, score yourself on evidence rather than identity: what did you ship, who used it twice, what did you learn from direct observation, whom did you do difficult work with, and what belief changed? That makes the year valuable whether you found immediately, join someone else, or decide startups are not for you.
