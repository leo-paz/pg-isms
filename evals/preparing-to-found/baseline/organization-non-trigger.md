# Organization Non-Trigger

Case ID: organization-non-trigger

Reviewer ID: preparing-baseline-001

## Frozen response

Redesign around clear outcomes and decision rights, not a more elaborate hierarchy. At 60 people, the company should have a small number of durable business or product areas, each with one accountable leader who can make most decisions without founder approval.

Start by defining roughly four to seven domains around customer or company outcomes—for example acquisition, core product, enterprise, platform, and operations—rather than functions that merely pass work to one another. Assign each domain a single directly responsible leader. That person owns its goals, budget envelope, staffing proposals, priorities, and operating results. Cross-functional specialists can still report to functional leaders for coaching and standards, but their day-to-day priorities should come from the domain team.

For every recurring decision type, publish who decides and what constraints apply. A lightweight decision-rights table should cover product priorities, pricing exceptions, hiring, spending, architecture, customer commitments, and incidents. Use four roles only where needed: proposer, consulted experts, decider, and executor. There must be exactly one decider. Consultation has a deadline and is not a veto unless the table explicitly grants one for legal, security, or financial risk.

Reserve founder decisions for a short list: company strategy, executive hiring or removal, financing, major acquisitions, existential brand or legal risks, and commitments above explicit financial thresholds. Everything else is delegated. If a founder disagrees with a delegated decision but it is inside the agreed guardrails, the decision stands. Otherwise employees will learn that ownership is fictional.

Change the coordination model as follows:

- Give each domain one or two quarterly outcomes with measurable indicators, rather than a long project list.
- Staff persistent cross-functional teams around those outcomes to reduce handoffs. A team should be able to discover, build, launch, and operate its work.
- Hold a weekly 30-minute domain review for decisions and risks, not status recitation. Written updates cover progress asynchronously.
- Hold a biweekly dependency forum only for issues that span domains. Each dependency gets an owner, due date, and explicit tradeoff.
- Keep functional councils for engineering, design, sales, and similar disciplines to set standards and develop people, but do not let councils become a second approval chain.
- Record consequential decisions in a short log: context, decider, decision, date, and review trigger.

Implement this over 60–90 days. In the first two weeks, map the last 20 consequential decisions and the handoffs in two or three important workflows. This reveals where authority is actually stuck. By week four, name domain leaders, define their outcome charters, and publish version one of the decision-rights table. In the next month, move people into persistent teams and have founders redirect out-of-scope approvals back to the named decider. In the final month, remove redundant meetings and adjust boundaries that repeatedly create dependencies.

Measure whether the redesign works through decision lead time, number of founder escalations, number and age of cross-team dependencies, cycle time from idea to customer impact, and employee answers to “Who can decide this?” Track quality guardrails too; faster bad decisions are not the goal.

The main behavioral requirement is founder restraint. For the first month, founders should publicly explain the new boundaries, coach leaders privately, and refuse to act as an informal appeals court. If a delegated leader repeatedly makes poor decisions, address the leader or the guardrails directly rather than reclaiming every decision.
