# Paying Users: Begin Now

Case ID: paying-users-begin-now

Reviewer ID: preparing-baseline-001

## Frozen response

Do not spend six months preparing in the abstract. You already have two things most “preparation” is meant to discover: a collaborator tested by years of shared work and prospective customers expressing urgent willingness to pay. Begin a disciplined company experiment now, while treating the customers’ behavior—not your excitement—as the test.

In the next seven days, meet each company separately and turn “we would pay” into specifics:

- What exact workflow or loss is urgent?
- Who owns the problem and budget?
- What is the smallest result they would pay for?
- What data, security, integration, procurement, and support requirements could block use?
- Will they sign a paid pilot, pay a deposit, or issue a purchase order now?

Propose a narrow 4–8 week paid pilot with a defined user group, deliverable, price, success measure, and end date. Avoid three custom consulting projects disguised as one product. If requirements diverge, choose the customer segment with the strongest common problem and say no to work that does not advance the shared product.

At the same time, have the cofounder conversation explicitly even though you know each other well. Write down roles, expected weekly commitment, when each person would leave employment, equity and vesting principles, how expenses are funded, decision areas, what happens if one person stops, and what evidence would cause you to continue or quit. Working well as teammates is excellent evidence, but founding adds financial risk, ambiguity, and authority questions that employment may have hidden.

Handle only the legal and operational basics needed to transact safely. Check both employers’ invention-assignment, moonlighting, confidentiality, and non-solicitation terms; keep code, accounts, time, and equipment separate. If the pilots require a company, form a simple entity, assign the product IP to it, agree to standard founder vesting, open a bank account, and use a clear pilot contract. Get targeted professional advice where jurisdiction, tax, employment, or customer data makes it necessary. Do not turn incorporation, branding, or fundraising into the work.

For the next six to eight weeks:

1. Secure at least one real financial commitment before expanding the product.
2. Work directly with users every week and observe the workflow rather than relying on feature requests.
3. Deliver the narrow result manually where useful, then automate repeated parts.
4. Track activation, repeated use, time or money saved, support load, sales-cycle friction, and whether the customer wants to renew or expand.
5. Continue interviewing similar companies so the product is not defined by the first three buyers alone.

Set a decision date and thresholds now. A reasonable continue signal would be multiple paid pilots in the same segment, repeated use by the intended users, a credible path from pilot to recurring purchase, and a problem important enough that customers tolerate a rough product. Warning signs include praise without signatures, three incompatible products, use only when you personally push it, impossible procurement economics, or one founder avoiding the work.

Do not both quit immediately merely to prove commitment. Use the next few weeks to convert urgency into contracts and learn the true delivery burden, subject to employer obligations. Once the evidence and runway justify it, choose a clear commitment transition—possibly one founder full-time first—rather than remaining indefinitely in nights-and-weekends limbo.

The correct posture is neither “prepare for six months” nor “bet everything tomorrow.” Start selling and delivering now, remove the few legal and founder-alignment risks that can kill the company, and let paid usage determine the size and speed of the commitment.
