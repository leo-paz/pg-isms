# Recommendation

The primary workflow should be cash survival and retention, not fundraising. Fundraising can run as a tightly bounded supporting workflow through September 30, but it must not control hiring, spending, or the operating forecast until money is legally committed and funded.

The reason is arithmetic. The company currently burns about $165,000 per month: $260,000 of cash expense less $95,000 of collected revenue. With $720,000 in cash, that is only about 4.4 months of runway at the current trend, before allowing for collection timing, severance, legal costs, or surprises. Revenue has not grown for six months, and 4.1% monthly logo churn makes the existing base fragile. The possible $3 million lead has neither a term sheet nor an approved partner decision. The $600,000 of angel interest is nonbinding. Neither belongs in the base case.

Accordingly, the three planned hires should be paused now. Continuing them would turn an uncertain financing process into a wager on verbal interest. Existing payroll and taxes remain the first protected obligations.

## Operating workflow: now through September 30

By July 22, the CEO and finance owner should establish a weekly 13-week cash forecast with three cases: no financing and no renewal uplift; both renewals retained; and financing actually funded. The forecast should use cash collection dates, not bookings. It should show payroll, taxes, vendor payments, renewal outcomes, and one-time costs. Every Monday, leadership should compare actual cash with the forecast and update the estimated zero-cash date.

Also by July 22, freeze hiring, new contractors, discretionary travel, and nonessential software or marketing commitments. Review every expense line and assign an owner to produce reductions that extend runway without impairing the two renewals or the retention test. The board should receive a written survival plan, including thresholds for deeper reductions. If reducing headcount may become necessary, obtain employment advice early and reserve enough cash for final pay, taxes, and related obligations rather than waiting until the bank balance forces a rushed decision.

Customer retention is the highest-leverage operating work. The two August 10 renewals represent $28,000 MRR, nearly 30% of current monthly collected revenue. Each account needs an executive owner, an account-specific renewal plan, a clear view of product and service blockers, and scheduled decision meetings before August 10. The company should not disguise discounts or extraordinary service costs; it should model the cash effect of any concession.

The founder-led retention test should continue with a fixed scope and an August 24 decision date. Define its success measures before launch: customers contacted, engagement, saves, contraction avoided, and a credible leading indicator for churn. Do not declare victory from anecdotes. On August 24, either operationalize the intervention with a named owner and modest budget, revise it based on evidence, or stop it.

Set explicit triggers. If either major renewal is lost, collections fall materially below forecast, or financing remains without a term sheet after the venture firm's August partner meeting, immediately activate the next expense reduction rather than simply revising the runway slide. By September 1, the board should approve a plan that preserves a minimum cash buffer and does not depend on a September financing close. By September 30, staffing and expense levels should be supportable by the no-financing case.

## What the capital workflow may do

The capital workflow may prepare truthful materials, test investor interest, conduct meetings, organize diligence, negotiate, and close financing. It may not authorize the three hires, count soft circles as cash, present the venture firm as committed, or defer necessary reductions merely to maintain fundraising optics.

The bridge deck should be concise and candid. It should cover the product and customer, current $95,000 monthly collected revenue, six months of flat performance, 4.1% monthly logo churn, $260,000 monthly cash expense, current cash and runway, the two pending renewals, the retention experiment, the cost-reset plan, proposed use of funds, and milestones that financing would buy. It should clearly distinguish historical facts, current commitments, and projections. The financing story cannot be "three hires will restart growth." It must explain what was learned from the stalled growth, why retention can improve, and how much runway the company purchases under a disciplined expense plan.

Build the target list in tiers. First are the prospective lead and investors already familiar with the company. Second are existing shareholders, the four angels, and funds that make bridge or seed-extension investments in collaboration software at this stage. Third are strategic or opportunistic investors who can move within the available timetable. For each, track relationship owner, check size, lead capability, conflicts, next action, and decision date. Avoid a broad, distracting outreach campaign if the company cannot support diligence.

Do not anchor on a single valuation range before testing structure and investor demand. Prepare a board-approved range based on dilution, ownership, runway purchased, and realistic current traction, along with alternatives such as a priced round, SAFE, or convertible note. Any quoted range should be labeled preliminary and subject to terms. Compare proposals on total economics: valuation or cap, discount, interest, maturity, liquidation preference, pro rata rights, board or veto rights, information rights, closing conditions, legal cost, and certainty of funding. A nominally higher valuation can be worse if control terms or closing risk are severe.

Run a dated process. By late July, finish the deck, financial model, cap table, corporate records, customer metrics, and diligence room. Secure specific meetings and ask the venture firm what it needs before its August partner meeting. Ask each angel to state the amount, conditions, and whether it will invest without the venture lead; continue to call every amount a soft circle until documents are signed and funds arrive. In August, pursue parallel lead and insider paths, provide consistent weekly updates, and seek written terms. In September, negotiate documents, complete approvals and diligence, and close only when the terms serve the company.

The target can be a September close, but management should not promise it. If there is no credible lead, written terms, and executable closing path by a board-set September checkpoint, the company should shift attention further toward survival alternatives, including a smaller insider financing, deeper expense reductions, strategic discussions, or an orderly wind-down plan. The correct outcome is not "raise at any price"; it is preserving options while avoiding insolvency and misrepresentation.
