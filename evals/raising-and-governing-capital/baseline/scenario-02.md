# Decision: choose Offer A

On the facts given, I would choose Offer A. Offer B’s higher valuation is attractive as a headline, but it does not produce meaningfully less dilution from the financing itself, and its economic downside protections and governance terms are materially worse for the company and founders. The extra $2 million also does not appear to buy a better outcome: the company is default-alive, has substantial runway, and the board believes either check funds the same 24-month milestone.

This is a business comparison, not legal, tax, or investment advice. Before signing, the company should have qualified counsel model the definitive terms and confirm details that the summaries omit.

## Dilution and cash

Ignoring the option-pool changes, Offer A sells $5 million / $45 million post-money, or about 11.11% of the company. Offer B sells $7 million / $62 million post-money, or about 11.29%. Thus, B actually sells slightly more of the company in the financing, not less. The CEO is comparing pre-money valuation headlines rather than post-money ownership.

The pool treatment strengthens A’s advantage. A’s pool increase is funded post-money, so its dilution is shared by the post-financing holders, including the new investor. B’s pool increase is funded pre-money, which generally places that dilution on the existing holders before the investor buys in. The exact ownership difference cannot be calculated without the requested pool size, the current pool, and the fully diluted capitalization table. Those numbers should be obtained immediately. But based on the stated terms, the $55 million headline does not establish that B is less dilutive and may obscure the opposite result.

The company currently has 30 months of cash at the stated burn rate: $4.5 million divided by $150,000 per month. That simple calculation assumes burn remains constant and ignores revenue or other changes, but it shows that the company is not facing a near-term cash cliff. Since either financing supports the same 24-month milestone, B’s extra $2 million is not tied to a stated strategic benefit. More cash can provide insurance, but here it does not compensate for much harsher terms unless management can identify a concrete use for it.

## Exit economics and future financing risk

Offer A’s 1x non-participating preference gives the investor a choice between recovering its $5 million preference and converting to common to share in the upside. It does not let the investor do both.

Offer B’s 1x participating preference gives the investor its $7 million back first and then lets it participate in the remaining proceeds, assuming there is no unstated cap. That can materially reduce what common holders receive in modest or middling exits. For example, using only the headline ownership and ignoring the pool, at a $40 million sale B could receive $7 million plus roughly 11.29% of the remaining $33 million, or about $10.7 million. A would take the better of its $5 million preference or roughly 11.11% on conversion, making $5 million the better result at that exit value. Actual waterfalls may differ, but the example shows why equal “1x” labels do not make the preferences equivalent.

The anti-dilution terms also diverge sharply. A’s broad-based weighted-average adjustment responds to a down round based on both price and amount raised. B’s full ratchet can reset the investor’s conversion price to the new lower price regardless of how small the financing is. If the company misses its plan and later raises at a lower valuation, B could impose substantial additional dilution on founders, employees, and other common holders. That risk is especially relevant because the references describe pressure from B after missed plans.

## Control and working relationship

A preserves a straightforward founder-controlled board: two founder seats and one investor seat. B creates a five-person board with two founder seats, two investor seats, and an independent who becomes the swing vote. Mutual approval of the independent is useful, but the founders would no longer control board outcomes on their own.

B’s vetoes go beyond a few extraordinary protective matters. Budget approval, executive hiring or firing, new debt, and any sale touch routine planning, leadership, financing flexibility, and exit decisions. Even without formal board control, those rights give B significant negative control over how the company operates. The veto over hiring or firing executives is particularly consequential when paired with two reports that B pressured companies to replace CEOs after missed plans. References do not prove what B would do here, but they are directly relevant evidence about behavior under stress. A’s consistently positive references reduce that relationship risk.

Governance terms often matter most when performance is below plan, precisely when economic protections such as full-ratchet anti-dilution can also activate. B concentrates both kinds of leverage in the same investor. That combination is more important than the difference between a $40 million and $55 million pre-money headline.

## Recommended course before July 27

The company should tell A that it is the preferred counterparty and move promptly toward signature, subject to confirming the complete term sheet and capitalization model. It should clarify the exact option-pool increase, any investor protective provisions not listed, voting thresholds, preference seniority, and whether any terms survive termination. It can also ask A whether the amount or other company-friendly terms can be improved, but should not put the acceptable offer at risk merely to chase B’s headline.

If management remains interested in B, the minimum commercial changes would be to remove participation, replace the full ratchet with broad-based weighted-average protection, shift the pool increase post-money, and narrow vetoes to genuinely extraordinary actions. The board structure and CEO-related references would still require comfort. Unless B materially revises that package, A offers the better balance of dilution, downside economics, founder control, and partner quality. The decision is therefore Offer A.
