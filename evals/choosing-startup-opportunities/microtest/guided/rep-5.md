# Two-week opportunity decision plan

Do not let ad clicks choose the company. The three options are not equivalent hypotheses: clicks would measure message response, not whether a named user has a costly problem, will change behavior, or can safely adopt the product.

Give the candidates these initial dispositions, dated for the end of week two:

- **C — deepen.** Named users perform an observed weekly workaround; the founders have direct experience and access; four controllers offer a discriminating commitment to pay for a pilot. The wedge is invoice reconciliation for a narrowly specified logistics-controller workflow. The remaining risks are whether a crude version saves enough time, fits the existing workflow, and can expand beyond the first companies.
- **B — stop for now.** It has no direct problem evidence or hospital access, and false output can harm patients. Reopen only if the team gains a qualified clinical partner and can define a reversible, monitored, non-clinical workflow or staged safety evaluation. A conference anecdote is not enough.
- **A — discard unless new firsthand evidence appears.** Funding news and imitation supply neither a named user nor an observed problem. Its falsifier is failure to find an urgent recurring meal-planning problem and users already making costly workarounds; do not spend acquisition money to manufacture evidence.

Days 1–2: write one opportunity record per option: named user, observed problem and workaround, frequency/cost, founder access and capability, smallest useful wedge, adoption burden, why-now evidence, plausible expansion, disqualifiers, and cheapest decisive test. Freeze the above provisional dispositions before collecting more evidence.

Days 3–5: concentrate on C. Have the eleven controllers walk through recent reconciliations using real, de-identified artifacts. Record exception types, time spent, error consequences, systems touched, approval constraints, and what they refuse to change. Confirm that the four pilot commitments mean an actual price, authorized buyer, start date, data/access requirements, and cancellation conditions—not polite interest. For B, spend at most one bounded access check seeking a clinical and compliance owner; do no patient-facing test. For A, run only a small problem-discovery check among people with recurring costly workarounds, not an ad campaign.

Days 6–10: test C's smallest useful wedge with a manual or narrowly instrumented reconciliation prototype inside one real workflow. Predefine the learning gate: at least four of the eleven provide representative cases; at least two authorized teams begin a paid or deposit-backed pilot; the prototype materially reduces handling time or errors on the selected exception class; and no integration or control requirement makes the wedge impractical. Track failures and adoption effort, not clicks. Spend only what this sample recruitment and reversible test require, not the proposed $30,000.

On day 10, review against the frozen gates. Deepen C if it passes. If it does not, identify whether the failure is the problem, segment, wedge, access, or delivery assumption; preserve the observed workflows and test the nearest supported adjustment rather than jumping to A or B. Stop C if payment commitments dissolve or the workflow cannot be improved cheaply enough.

The ordered next handoff is: **`learning-from-users` first**, with the hypothesis “logistics controllers who reconcile recurring invoice exceptions will adopt and pay for a narrow tool that reduces handling time or errors,” the eleven controllers as the sample, the explicit thresholds above, and a day-10 review. Only after real use, payment, or the predeclared equivalent commitment clears that gate should primary ownership pass to **`shipping-and-iterating-products`** for the selected wedge, then **`designing-business-models`** for pricing and sales architecture, and finally **`acquiring-and-growing-users`** for repeatable acquisition and expansion.
