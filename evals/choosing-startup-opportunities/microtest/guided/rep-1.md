# Two-week decision plan

Choose C as the direction to deepen. Treat all three as hypotheses, but do not let ad clicks choose the company.

## Initial dispositions

- **A — discard:** funding news and clicks show fashion, not an observed user problem. Reopen only if the team finds a named user, costly workaround, and founder access that make a cheap demand test possible.
- **B — stop:** the signal is second-hand, the team lacks hospital access, and errors can harm patients. Do not expose patients or build a diagnostic prototype. Reopen only with a clinical partner, access to the real workflow, a clearly bounded non-harmful wedge, and a safety/regulatory test plan using monitored or staged evidence.
- **C — deepen:** two founders know the work, eleven controllers repeat it weekly, and four have expressed willingness to pay. Its first wedge is reconciliation of the recurring invoice workflow for logistics controllers; the expansion path can remain a risk until this wedge earns use and payment.

## Days 1–3: freeze the learning gate

Hand C to `learning-from-users` with this written hypothesis: “Logistics controllers who reconcile invoices weekly will grant workflow/data access and pay for a narrow reconciliation pilot because the current process consumes enough time and creates costly exceptions.”

Sample the four prospective payers plus at least four of the other seven controllers. Observe the workflow rather than asking whether the idea sounds good. Record time, exception volume, errors, approvals, existing tools, switching constraints, data sensitivity, and who controls purchasing. Ask for the smallest safe data set needed for a pilot.

Set the day-14 evidence threshold before testing: at least three independent controllers provide representative data, sign a paid pilot or make an equivalent hard commitment, and agree on a measurable success criterion. Require the pilot to show a material reduction in reconciliation time or missed exceptions without adding an unacceptable review burden. A verbal promise, survey answer, or click does not count.

## Days 4–11: run the smallest useful test

Use a manual or concierge workflow behind the scenes to reconcile a bounded invoice batch and return a reviewable exception list. Keep a human in control; do not spend the $30,000 on broad ads or a polished product. Spend only what is needed for secure data handling and the pilot. Measure baseline versus pilot time, exceptions found, correction work, repeat use, and whether payment is actually authorized.

In parallel, write the dated opportunity record for C: current workaround and cost, founder access and capability, smallest wedge, adoption effort, timing change if any, disqualifiers, and plausible adjacent workflows. Preserve A and B as short records so their evidence is not erased.

## Days 12–14: decide

- **Deepen C** if the precommitted access, payment, and workflow-improvement threshold is met. Name remaining integration, timing, and expansion risks.
- **Stop or narrow C** if payment or data access fails despite real pain; distinguish a bad problem from a bad segment, wedge, access path, or delivery method before considering an adjacent workflow.
- **Discard C** if controllers will not make a hard commitment or the pilot cannot improve the workflow enough to overcome adoption effort.

The ordered handoff is: `choosing-startup-opportunities` selects C and its wedge; `learning-from-users` now owns the hypothesis, sample, threshold, and day-14 review; if that gate passes, `shipping-and-iterating-products` owns the smallest production version. Route pricing architecture to `designing-business-models` afterward, and acquisition to `acquiring-and-growing-users` only after real use or payment confirms the segment.
