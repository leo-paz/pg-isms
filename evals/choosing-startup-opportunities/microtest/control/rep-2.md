Days 1–3: interview the eleven controllers, observe at least five reconciliation sessions, and quantify frequency, time, error rates, workarounds, decision authority, and current spend. Ask the four prospective pilot customers to define what they would pay for and who can approve it.

Days 4–6: map the narrowest common workflow and list the assumptions that could kill the idea: data access, integration effort, accuracy, exception handling, buyer urgency, and price. Set a decision rule before building—for example, proceed only if at least eight controllers rank the problem among their top three operational pains, at least three pilot customers can supply representative data, and at least three sign a paid-pilot agreement.

Days 7–12: run a concierge pilot using real but appropriately protected data. Automate only enough to test the riskiest workflow step. Compare against the existing process on completion time, error rate, exception rate, setup burden, and operator trust. Charge a small but real pilot fee or secure a signed agreement with a start date and price.

Days 13–14: review the precommitted thresholds. Commit to C only if the workflow evidence, usable data, measurable improvement, and payment signal all hold; otherwise revise the narrow problem or stop without defaulting to A or B.

The ordered handoff is: first convert the validated workflow and pilot evidence into a tightly scoped initial product experiment; next, if the paid pilot repeats across customers, define the go-to-market motion around the actual controller or finance buyer.
