Choose Option C for the two-week test. It has firsthand founder knowledge, a repeated and observable problem, reachable users, and early willingness to pay. Park A until there is evidence of a real problem rather than market-fashion interest. Reject B for now: the team lacks access and the safety and regulatory risks make it a poor first learning loop. Do not spend the $30,000 on ads; clicks would test copy and targeting, not whether the team can solve a valuable workflow.

Two-week plan:

1. Days 1–3: Interview and observe the eleven controllers. Map the current reconciliation workflow, frequency, time spent, error costs, edge cases, existing tools, data-access constraints, and who controls the budget. Ask the four willing buyers what a paid pilot would need to accomplish.
2. Days 4–5: Define a narrow pilot around one common invoice-reconciliation job. Agree on baseline measures, including handling time, match accuracy, exceptions requiring human review, and willingness to pay. Use redacted or appropriately secured data.
3. Days 6–10: Run a concierge prototype for the four interested controllers, doing substantial work manually behind a minimal interface. Review results with them daily and record where the workflow fails.
4. Days 11–12: Ask for a concrete paid-pilot commitment at a proposed price and verify that the economic buyer, not just the user, agrees. Compare results with the baseline.
5. Days 13–14: Continue only if at least three customers commit to paying, the pilot materially reduces handling time without unacceptable errors, and the same core workflow recurs across customers. Otherwise narrow or abandon the opportunity based on the observed failure mode.

The ordered next handoff is: customer discovery and paid-pilot sales first; then product scoping and iterative building around the verified workflow; then repeatable customer acquisition only after retention and value are demonstrated. Fundraising and broad paid marketing come later, if they become necessary.
