# Raw held-out output

Case ID: offshore-inspection-pivot-transfer

Generator ID: choosing-heldout-generator-001

## Decision

For the next twelve months, pivot from autonomous blade repair to a narrow inspection-and-triage product for offshore-wind maintenance managers. Park the repair arm; do not pursue warehouse humanoids; reject the $250,000 broad-growth program.

The chosen problem is not “robotics for wind.” It is the repeated, costly decision workflow in which maintenance managers reconcile drone images, sensor exports, and contractor reports to decide which turbines warrant a vessel visit. The initial wedge is a tethered system that produces a traceable, manager-reviewable priority list from those inputs. Six of eight observed managers report the same workflow, it costs about $180,000 per site annually, three will provide data and supervised access, and one offers a conditional $40,000 evaluation. That is substantially stronger evidence than either an unpilotable repair robot or an unrelated fashionable market.

The founders also have an unusual right to test this direction: eight years of reliability experience, operator access, field knowledge, an imagery pipeline, and reusable software. Abandoning the arm is not erasing the first fourteen months; it preserves the users, workflow, constraints, relationships, and useful capabilities while dropping the part blocked by a two-year certification and insurance path.

## Candidate dispositions

- **Inspection and triage — deepen, reviewed monthly.** Falsify it if blinded customer data cannot meet a customer-specified safety threshold, if managers will not use the output in vessel-planning decisions, or if the team cannot convert supervised access into a paid evaluation.
- **Autonomous repair arm — stop for twelve months.** Preserve the test results and design files, but assign no product headcount. Reopen only if an operator and insurer jointly sponsor a defined certification path and a scheduled field pilot; technical bench performance alone is insufficient.
- **Warehouse humanoids — discard now.** The founders have no first-hand problem, access, workflow evidence, or differentiated capability there. Reconsider only upon acquiring real domain expertise and observing a specific urgent workflow—not because investor fashion persists.
- **Broad conferences and lead generation — stop now.** Before validation, growth may recruit the defined research sample and pilot beachhead only.

## Twelve-month gates

**Days 0–30: freeze the learning contract.** The domain founder owns customer access; the technical founder owns feasibility. `learning-from-users` owns the hypothesis: “Offshore-wind maintenance managers will use a traceable inspection-and-triage output to prioritize vessel visits, provided it stays below a false-negative ceiling they specify in advance.” Sign data-use terms with the three managers and the paid-evaluation terms with the fourth. Before viewing evaluation labels, define the defect classes, false-negative ceiling, comparison against the current manual process, sample, acceptance procedure, and review date. Record the current process's labor, elapsed time, and vessel-decision outputs. If customers will not precommit data, access, and acceptance rules, stop.

**Months 2–3: archived-data replay.** Build only the smallest ingestion, reconciliation, and traceable-priority pipeline; do not rebuild autonomous repair. Evaluate on held-out archived failures that were not used for tuning. Advance only if it meets the pre-agreed false-negative limit and materially reduces reconciliation time without hiding uncertainty. If it fails, diagnose whether the problem is data quality, model performance, or workflow fit; allow one bounded correction cycle, then stop if the same gate fails again.

**Months 4–6: supervised field workflow.** Run the tethered prototype at two sites with managers in the loop. The gate is not a successful demo: operators must actually use its output in a scheduled vessel-prioritization exercise, it must meet the safety threshold, and the workflow must be faster than the recorded baseline. Log overrides and their reasons. Any safety incident or systematic missed-defect class stops expansion and returns the system to offline work.

**Months 7–9: paid evaluation.** Deliver the contractually scoped $40,000 evaluation against the frozen acceptance procedure. Advance only upon payment and a written decision to continue, expand, or procure; praise, conference interest, and unpaid pilots do not count. Until this gate, growth owns only recruiting comparable maintenance teams and maintaining the narrow relationship pipeline.

**Months 10–12: prove repeatability.** Seek two additional paid site evaluations in the same manager segment using substantially the same workflow and product. Require at least one repeat purchase, expanded site scope, or equivalent operational commitment from an existing user. If achieved, hand primary acquisition ownership to growth, route pricing and contract design to `designing-business-models`, and route the release plan to `shipping-and-iterating-products`. If not, keep learning ownership and decide whether to deepen the intense user subset, adjust the wedge, or stop.

## Boundaries and open risks

Cap twelve-month work at the triage wedge; treat automated repair as a possible later expansion, not as a promise. A credible path outward is fleet-wide condition monitoring, maintenance planning, and eventually repair support, but each step must earn its own adoption and regulatory evidence. The principal unresolved risks are the absence of a demonstrated “why now” beyond current customer access, cross-site data variability, insurer treatment of decision-support outputs, and whether one conditional payer represents a repeatable segment. Date every evidence review, and do not let sunk cost, robotics identity, or investor fashion override these gates.
