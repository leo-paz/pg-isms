# Decision memo

**Date:** May 4, 2026  
**To:** ArchiveFox leadership  
**Subject:** Six-week delegation of implementation exceptions

## Decision

ArchiveFox should immediately run a six-week, reversible delegation pilot. The Head of Implementation should own routine go-live sequencing and data-mapping exceptions that fit documented precedents. The CEO should stop approving those cases individually and retain authority over novel or material commitments, including new security or contract terms, unsupported product or data-retention promises, and service credits above $8,000.

The present constraint is decision ownership, not demonstrated staffing capacity. Twenty of 48 first-quarter requests waited more than two business days; 15 of those 20 were routine choices matching previously accepted patterns. Nine delayed go-lives, and unclear ownership caused six contradictory dates. After a decision, 17 of the 20 delayed cases were completed without overtime or added staff. Utilization averaged 69% and post-decision backlog remained near 1.2 days. The shadow exercise also provides limited but useful evidence: the Head of Implementation matched the CEO in 16 of 18 cases, with the two differences occurring at sensible boundaries for retained authority.

## Authority and guardrails

On May 4, the CEO and Head of Implementation should publish a one-page decision-rights table, accessible to Implementation and Customer Success:

- The Head of Implementation decides routine sequencing and mapping deviations when the proposed action matches a recorded precedent, does not change signed terms, creates no new security or retention obligation, and does not promise unsupported functionality.
- The Head may approve a service credit of no more than $8,000 only when it follows an existing approved pattern and does not alter contract language. Any novel credit structure, amount above $8,000, or doubt about precedent goes to the CEO.
- New security obligations go directly to the existing security adviser; new or changed contract terms go directly to counsel. The CEO remains accountable for the resulting business decision.
- Any unsupported retention or product promise, unusual customer commitment, or case without adequate documentation is outside delegated authority.
- When classification is uncertain, the default is escalation—not an improvised customer promise.

Every exception gets one accountable owner. Implementation owns implementation decisions; Customer Success communicates only the date and commitment recorded by that owner. Neither function may infer that the other has decided. Existing customer contacts and approved plans remain in place, and the pilot does not reopen prior commitments.

## Six-week operating plan: May 4–June 14

During the first two business days, leadership should classify all open exceptions, document the accepted sequencing and mapping precedents, assign each item an owner, and repair the request with no usable record. The tracker should require the request, customer impact, deadline, proposed decision, precedent relied upon, decision owner, escalation destination, final decision, and customer message.

For each new request, the owner should acknowledge it within four business hours and either decide or escalate it within one business day. An escalation must include a recommendation and the precise approval needed. The CEO should designate coverage for retained decisions during expected unavailability. If no authorized approver can respond before a customer deadline, ArchiveFox should preserve the last approved plan, make no new commercial, legal, security, or product promise, and give the customer a specific time for the next update.

The Head of Implementation and Customer Success lead should hold a brief daily review of only blocked or newly escalated cases. A weekly leadership review should inspect patterns and boundary breaches; it should not re-approve completed routine decisions. Customer-facing changes should come from the recorded decision, through the existing relationship owner, so customers receive one date and one explanation.

Track weekly:

- exception volume and type;
- time to owner assignment and decision;
- requests waiting more than two business days;
- go-live delays attributable to decision waits;
- contradictory customer dates;
- delegated decisions later reversed by the CEO;
- credits by count and dollar value;
- security, contract, retention, and unsupported-feature escalations;
- customer complaints or adverse effects associated with the pilot;
- utilization, backlog, overtime, and added staffing.

Any unauthorized promise, bypass of security or counsel, repeated contradictory dates, or material customer harm should trigger an immediate review of the relevant authority rather than automatic cancellation of the entire pilot.

## What to defer or reject

Do not hire a COO now. The proposed role has neither a charter nor a costed capacity model, and possible growth to 25 people next year does not establish a current need. The evidence instead supports fixing decision rights with the people already in place. Reconsider an operations hire only after defining the work, showing sustained capacity or coordination demand, and comparing that demand with the cost and runway effect.

Reject the weekly cross-functional approval council as a decision body. It would add a batch delay and blur the single-owner model. The weekly retrospective above is sufficient for learning and control. Also do not revive the twice-daily CEO Slack queue; it did not reduce waits, and the CEO's expected unavailability makes queue discipline an inadequate substitute for delegation.

## June 15 decision

Leadership should review the complete tracker and choose among three outcomes. Continue the authority unchanged if routine decisions are timely, reversals and boundary breaches are rare, customer continuity is intact, and backlog, utilization, and overtime remain stable. Narrow or pause specific authorities if the pilot produces material customer harm, repeated misclassification, unauthorized commitments, or unmanageable credit exposure. Expand delegation only where repeated cases show a clear precedent and safe decisions; do not expand security, contract, retention, or unsupported-product authority merely to improve speed.

If delays persist mainly in retained CEO decisions, leadership should first redesign coverage and define the residual operating role. Only then should it evaluate a COO or another role against a written charter, capacity model, compensation cost, and runway impact.

The evidence has limits: the shadow sample contains only 18 cases, the tracker has one unusable record, and the data cover one quarter. The three-week shift in the start of $42,000 ARR shows timing exposure, not necessarily permanent revenue loss. These caveats support a monitored, reversible pilot rather than either permanent broad delegation or another executive hire today.
