# Decision memo

**Date:** May 4, 2026  
**To:** ArchiveFox leadership  
**Subject:** Six-week pilot for bounded implementation authority

## Decision

ArchiveFox should immediately run a six-week pilot that gives the Head of Implementation final authority over routine go-live sequencing and data-mapping exceptions when the proposed action matches a documented precedent and does not change a customer’s contract, security posture, retention terms, product commitments, or service credits above $8,000.

This is bounded delegation, not general permission to make unusual promises. The evidence supports it: 15 of the 20 delayed requests were routine choices matching accepted patterns; in the shadow exercise, the Head’s recommendation matched the CEO’s in 16 of 18 cases; and the two differences identify useful boundaries. The team’s stable post-decision backlog and lack of overtime in 17 completed cases suggest the immediate constraint is decision latency, not demonstrated staffing capacity.

The CEO should retain authority for credits above $8,000 and any novel or unsupported customer promise. New security or contract terms must continue to go directly to the security adviser or counsel. Data-retention changes should be treated as contractual or security-sensitive unless the existing written terms and supported product behavior clearly cover them. No silence or missed deadline should ever constitute approval for one of these retained matters.

Customer Success may supply customer context and urgency, but the tracker must name exactly one decision owner. For pilot-eligible matters, that owner is the Head of Implementation. This removes the current ambiguity without creating a committee.

## Explicitly defer or reject

- **Defer hiring a COO.** Possible growth to 25 people next year is not enough evidence to create the role now, particularly without a charter or costed capacity model. Revisit only after identifying a persistent leadership-capacity problem that bounded decision rights do not solve and defining the role, outcomes, cost, and runway effect.
- **Reject a weekly cross-functional approval council for this problem.** It would replace one approval queue with another and could hold urgent requests for up to a week. A short retrospective may inspect decisions, but it must not become a required approval step.
- **Reject broad or informal delegation.** The Head of Implementation may not alter security, legal, contractual, retention, or unsupported product commitments, nor approve credits above the pilot threshold.
- **Do not repeat the twice-daily CEO Slack queue.** It did not reduce waits and does not address the CEO’s expected unavailability. Slack can notify people, but the tracker, named owner, and clock govern the work.

## Six-week operating model: May 5–June 15

During the first two business days, the CEO and Head of Implementation should convert previously accepted sequencing and mapping patterns into a short written decision-rights table. Each entry should state the eligible situation, permitted response, prohibited changes, and escalation owner. This is a working boundary for the pilot, not an attempt to document every possible case.

Every new exception must have one tracker record containing: customer, request time, requested decision date, category, proposed action, cited precedent if any, customer/go-live effect, named decision owner, escalation destination, decision time, decision, and rationale. No decision is complete until recorded; the unrecorded historical case makes this control material.

Use the following clock:

1. Within four business hours, the Head of Implementation classifies the request, names the owner, and gives Implementation and Customer Success one shared status and expected decision time.
2. A routine, precedent-backed sequencing or mapping request is decided by the Head within one business day of receipt.
3. A retained matter is routed immediately to the CEO, security adviser, or counsel as applicable, with the facts and a recommendation. The customer-facing owner communicates a realistic next update rather than inventing a decision date.
4. If the retained owner does not answer by the required customer milestone, the affected novel term or promise remains unapproved. The Head may continue unaffected implementation work and may use an existing supported, contract-compliant option when one is already documented; otherwise, the team pauses only the affected exception and communicates the constraint and next update to the customer.

The CEO should not pre-approve individual routine cases. Instead, the CEO should audit the first five pilot decisions and then a weekly sample, recording whether the classification and result were acceptable. Any disagreement should update the decision-rights table prospectively. An actual safety-boundary breach can suspend the relevant category immediately; ordinary disagreement should not retroactively create a second approval gate.

Leadership should review a compact dashboard once a week. It should show request volume by category; time to classification and decision; percentage of routine cases decided within one business day; requests waiting over two business days; go-lives delayed awaiting a decision; contradictory customer dates; CEO audit disagreements; credits and other retained matters; security/counsel routing; unsupported promises; and missing records. It should also track post-decision backlog, utilization, overtime or added staffing, and any observed customer impact. The weekly meeting is for learning and boundary maintenance, not case approval.

## Decision after six weeks

On June 16, the CEO, Head of Implementation, and Customer Success lead should review the complete tracker and decide among making the authority permanent, narrowing or revising it for another time-boxed pilot, or returning a category to the CEO.

Make the delegation permanent only if all safety controls held—no unauthorized security, contract, retention, product, or above-threshold credit commitments; all such cases routed correctly; and no missing decision records—and operating results meet these targets:

- at least 90% of eligible routine requests decided within one business day;
- no contradictory dates caused by unclear ownership;
- no go-live delayed solely while an eligible routine decision awaited approval; and
- no material deterioration in customer outcomes, post-decision backlog, utilization, or overtime attributable to the pilot.

If the pilot is safe but misses speed or continuity targets, revise the categories, precedents, or clock and run another bounded pilot. If a material boundary breach occurs, narrow or pause the affected authority and determine why before resuming. If results are strong, expand authority only one adjacent, clearly defined category at a time with the same controls.

Six weeks is a small sample, and the April shadow exercise tested recommendations rather than live accountability. The review should therefore treat the results as evidence for the next bounded decision, not proof that all implementation authority can safely move away from the CEO.
