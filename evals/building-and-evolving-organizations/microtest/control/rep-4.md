# Decision memo

**Date:** May 4, 2026  
**To:** ArchiveFox leadership  
**Subject:** Six-week implementation-decision pilot

## Decision

ArchiveFox should immediately delegate a narrow class of implementation exceptions to the Head of Implementation for a six-week live pilot. The delegated class should be limited to go-live sequencing and data-mapping choices that match a previously accepted pattern and do not alter price, credit, contract, security, supported product behavior, data retention, or another customer-facing commitment.

This is a targeted removal of a demonstrated bottleneck, not general authority to make unusual promises. Fifteen of the twenty requests that waited more than two business days were routine choices matching precedent. In the shadow exercise, the Head independently matched the CEO in 16 of 18 cases; the two differences identify the boundary to preserve, not a reason to keep all decisions centralized. The team also completed 17 of the 20 delayed items without overtime or new staff once a decision arrived, while utilization and the post-decision backlog do not indicate that delivery capacity is the main constraint.

The CEO should approve the pilot charter and authority matrix today, then stop re-approving in-scope decisions before they are executed. The Head of Implementation owns those decisions and their record. Customer Success owns customer communication but may not create a second decision path.

## Boundaries and continuity

The Head may decide only when all of the following are true:

- the choice is sequencing or data mapping;
- the decision cites at least one usable prior accepted pattern;
- it stays within the current contract, documented product support, security posture, data-retention terms, and approved customer plan; and
- it creates no service credit, discount, new legal term, or unusual customer promise.

Anything outside those conditions is escalated. New security terms continue to go promptly to the security adviser, and new contract terms to counsel, as they do now. The CEO retains service credits and any deviation involving economics or a new customer promise during the pilot. In particular, the pilot does not authorize a credit above $8,000 or any unsupported data-retention promise; the shadow exercise shows those are live failure modes. ArchiveFox should not infer that credits below $8,000 are safe to delegate merely because the observed disagreement was above that amount.

When ownership is uncertain, the default is escalation, not invention. Existing agreed dates and terms remain in force until an authorized decision changes them. Customer Success sends one coordinated status to the customer, stating what remains unchanged, who owns the pending decision, and the next update time. This preserves continuity without making a premature promise.

## Six-week operating model

Run the pilot from May 4 through June 12, with a written closeout immediately afterward.

Use one shared exception register as the system of record. Every request receives a unique owner and timestamp. The record must contain: customer, requested decision, current committed date, decision category, precedent cited, authority owner, time received, decision and time, customer communication owner, and outcome. Missing records are treated as a process failure; the unusable record in the prior quarter makes this control necessary.

At intake, the Head of Implementation classifies each request as either delegated or retained. Customer Success may challenge a classification but may not open a parallel approval path. For a delegated request, the Head acknowledges it within four business hours and decides within one business day. For a retained request, the Head routes it to the named owner within four business hours and gives Customer Success a next-update time. If a retained request has no response after one business day, it is visibly escalated to the CEO; no one treats silence as approval. Because the CEO expects to be unavailable roughly ten hours each week, leadership should publish those windows and use the safe default—hold existing commitments—rather than rely on another Slack queue. The March twice-daily queue already failed to reduce waits.

For the first two weeks, the CEO reviews delegated decisions after execution twice weekly. This is an audit, not a second approval. For weeks three through six, continue weekly review of every delegated decision; expected volume is small enough that sampling is unnecessary. Any unauthorized promise, missed security or legal escalation, or customer-impacting error triggers an immediate boundary review. It does not automatically invalidate unrelated routine delegation.

Leadership should review a compact dashboard each week:

- routine requests decided within one business day and requests waiting more than two business days;
- go-lives delayed by decision latency and contradictory dates issued;
- CEO post-decision reversals and the reason for each;
- unauthorized commitments, missed escalations, service credits, and customer complaints attributable to a decision;
- implementation backlog and utilization, to detect whether the constraint has moved; and
- completeness of the exception register.

## Explicit deferrals and rejections

Do not hire a COO now on the evidence provided. ArchiveFox has no role charter or costed capacity model, and a possible increase from 16 to 25 people next year does not establish the work, timing, or economics of that hire. Defer the question until the pilot reveals which decisions remain bottlenecked and leadership has written the role, quantified the workload, and compared hiring with clearer existing-role authority.

Reject a weekly cross-functional approval council for this pilot. It would batch decisions, blur individual accountability, and risk recreating the delay already caused by uncertain ownership. Use a brief retrospective for learning, not a committee for case approval. Also do not repeat the twice-daily CEO Slack queue or delegate all implementation exceptions as one undifferentiated category.

## June decision

Before launch, leadership should adopt the following pilot success conditions: at least 90% of delegated routine requests decided within one business day; no contradictory customer dates; no unauthorized economic, legal, security, retention, or product commitments; no customer-impacting incident caused by a delegated decision; complete records; and no more than 10% of delegated decisions substantively reversed by the CEO. These are governance thresholds chosen for the pilot, not facts established by the historical data.

After June 12, the CEO, Head of Implementation, and Customer Success should review the full register against those conditions. If they are met and decision latency materially improves from the January–March baseline, make the authority permanent and consider expanding only one boundary at a time through another measured pilot. If results are mixed, keep the proven categories and tighten the failing boundary. If customer harm, unauthorized commitments, or repeated poor decisions occur, suspend the affected authority and diagnose the pattern before retrying.

The evidence is promising but limited: the shadow exercise covered only 18 hypothetical decisions, exposed no customer to the choices, and the historical record includes one unusable case. The six-week design therefore treats delegation as a reversible operating experiment with hard promise boundaries, full traceability, and explicit continuation criteria.
