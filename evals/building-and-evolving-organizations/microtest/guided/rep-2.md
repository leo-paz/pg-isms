# Decision memo: implementation exception authority

**Date:** May 4, 2026  
**Decision horizon:** May 4-June 15, 2026  
**Decision:** Run a six-week, reversible delegation pilot. The Head of Implementation will own routine implementation sequencing and data-mapping exceptions within documented precedents. Preserve independent security/legal review and CEO approval for credits, novel contractual or security/privacy terms, unsupported product or retention promises, and other exceptions outside the written boundary. Do not hire a COO or create a weekly approval council now.

**Primary route and branch:** Building and evolving organizations; `decision-rights`. The Head of Implementation owns on-time, supportable go-lives. The CEO owns the pilot boundary and retained commercial/strategic risks. The existing security adviser and counsel retain qualified review in their domains. Customer Success owns accurate customer communication after a decision, not exception approval. Intermediate reviews are May 18 and June 1; the CEO makes the final decision on June 15 from the written record.

## State, evidence, and gates

ArchiveFox is a 16-person enterprise B2B SaaS company serving 31 museums, at $1.7 million ARR with 20 months of runway. Default-dead status, profitability, release state, and contractual exposure are unknown, so this memo does not make a survival claim. The work is customer implementation: decision latency is short, routine choices are reversible, but security, contract, privacy, unsupported promises, credits, and customer continuity have higher error costs and keep their current protections.

**Observed:** From January 1-March 31, 48 exception requests were recorded; 20 waited over two business days (3.4-day median), nine delayed a go-live, and six created contradictory dates. Fifteen delayed requests were routine choices matching accepted patterns. Four novel security or contract matters reached the existing adviser or counsel promptly; one request lacks a usable record. After CEO decisions, 17 of 20 were completed without overtime or added staff. One delay shifted the start of $42,000 ARR by three weeks. In the April 6-17 shadow exercise, the Head of Implementation independently matched 16 of 18 CEO decisions; the differences were a credit above $8,000 and an unsupported data-retention promise. Utilization averaged 69%, and post-decision backlog stayed near 1.2 days. A twice-daily CEO Slack queue did not reduce waits, and the CEO expects about ten unavailable hours weekly.

**Claimed:** The CEO believes delegation could cause promises to spiral; the Head of Implementation believes routine calls are manageable but boundaries are unclear; Customer Success wants a reliable clock. The investor's forecast of 25 people next year and recommendation to hire a COO and add a council are advice, not evidence of a present role or capacity gap.

**Unknown:** Whether shadow accuracy persists with live consequences; whether the proposed boundary prevents unauthorized commitments; sustainable decision load; hidden rework; why the Slack queue failed; the contents of the unrecorded request; and whether any residual capacity gap remains after delegation. The Head of Implementation will maintain the pilot log; Customer Success will reconcile customer dates; the CEO will audit outcomes. By June 15, these artifacts must show the thresholds below. Counsel or the security adviser, as applicable, must confirm by May 8 that the written escalation boundary preserves existing review; if not, the relevant category stays under the prior approval path.

## Bottleneck and chosen change

The outcome at risk is timely, internally consistent go-live execution. The current path sends every exception to the CEO, producing recurring delay and unclear ownership. The primary mechanism is centralized routine authority combined with CEO unavailability. Rivals are incomplete request records, cross-functional handoff confusion, or downstream capacity. The failed Slack queue suggests queue frequency alone is insufficient; the 69% utilization, 1.2-day post-decision backlog, and 17-of-20 completions without added labor argue against current implementation capacity as the leading cause. The hypothesis is falsified if routine decision time improves but go-live delay or contradictory dates do not.

The smallest intervention is clearer authority, not headcount or another forum. It costs management time to define precedents and audit decisions and risks boundary errors, but it is reversible and avoids recruiting, compensation, ramp, another layer, extra handoffs, and diluted outcome ownership. Potential relief is fewer delayed go-lives and less CEO dependency; no revenue recovery beyond the documented delayed $42,000 ARR start is assumed.

Effective May 6, the Head of Implementation may decide an exception only when all of the following are true: it concerns sequencing or mapping; a cited prior accepted pattern exists; it does not change price, credit, contract, security, privacy, retention, product capability, support obligation, or another customer promise; and it does not create a material conflict across customers. The decision log must link the request, precedent, decision, rationale, owner, timestamp, communicated date, and outcome.

Anything outside that boundary is escalated immediately. Security/privacy matters go to the existing security adviser; legal or contract terms go to counsel; service credits, unsupported promises, novel commercial terms, or unclear cases go to the CEO. These reviewers, not the Head of Implementation, decide within their remit. The CEO will publish availability and name an absence backup by May 5; until a qualified backup is named, the prior protected approval remains in force and the team must not promise an answer or date it cannot support.

Customer Success supplies customer context by noon of the next business day and, after the logged decision, communicates the single authoritative date. It may not create a competing go-live date. Routine requests must be acknowledged the same business day and decided by the end of the next business day. Escalated requests must be routed the same business day with a named owner and expected response time; silence never becomes approval. The Head of Implementation is the implementation continuity owner; the CEO is backup for routine authority and succession owner. If both are unavailable, the last supportable plan remains in effect and no new exception is promised.

The May 18 and June 1 reviews sample every decision, compare it with the boundary and precedent, reconcile customer communications, and inspect rework, backlog, utilization, go-live effects, and escalation response. This is outcome review, not renewed preapproval.

## June 15 decision rules

- **Keep current scope:** at least 90% of eligible routine requests are decided within one business day; none delays a go-live solely awaiting routine approval; there are zero contradictory customer dates, unauthorized commitments, or bypasses of security/counsel/CEO gates; 100% of requests have usable records; and post-decision backlog remains at or below two business days without overtime or added staff.
- **Revise once:** the delegation mechanism works but exactly one boundary, interface, clock, or workload assumption misses without customer or protected-domain harm. Change that one variable, name its owner, and set one final review no later than June 29.
- **Expand:** only if all keep thresholds pass, extend delegation to the next specifically named, precedent-backed implementation category; document its exclusions, owner, backup, and review before use. Do not broadly delegate commercial, legal, security/privacy, or unsupported-product commitments.
- **Add capacity:** only if, after the authority and interface changes, a sustained residual workload keeps backlog above two business days or causes missed go-lives while utilization and work records show a capacity or capability gap whose relief exceeds full role cost. First write and cost a role charter; this evidence would not automatically justify a COO.
- **Rollback or stop:** immediately restore CEO preapproval for the affected category if there is an unauthorized promise or credit, bypassed qualified review, material customer harm, missing accountability, or two out-of-bound delegated decisions. Preserve the records and send security/legal/customer consequences to the applicable qualified owner.

Explicitly defer the COO decision until a measured residual executive outcome, decision set, hands-on work, capacity gap, interfaces, cost, and 30/60/90-day evidence justify a charter. Reject the weekly cross-functional approval council now: it adds a recurring handoff to a queue whose current problem is authority, and it has no separate evidenced mechanism or owner. Preserve the option to open a scoped role or add the smallest capacity later if the June evidence meets the add-capacity gate.

Unresolved facts remain the company's survival status, hidden rework, the missing request record, sustainable live-decision accuracy, and actual escalation responsiveness. The Head of Implementation owns evidence completion by June 12; the CEO owns the June 15 branch decision; qualified reviewers retain their present protections throughout.

**Lineage:** The branch, evidence ledger, safeguards, and decision thresholds are taxonomy synthesis from the supplied organization runtime contract; no essay-derived mechanism or unverified external fact is asserted. Current legal, contractual, security, privacy, employment, or financial implications remain for the company's qualified advisers to verify.
