# Raw evaluation output

Case ID: scenario-05

Reviewer ID: positioning-baseline-002

Today the open model is primarily a complement: it can make one input to the product cheaper while leaving the workflow customers renew for—playbooks, exception routing, integrations, approvals, auditability, and compliance—unreplaced. It is not yet a direct substitute for the product. It is, however, a transition threat because extraction may cease to be a differentiator, model suppliers may move up the stack, and competitors can use the same lower-cost capability. The correct response is to absorb the commodity improvement and strengthen the workflow advantage, not pivot reflexively into capital-intensive model training.

Weeks 1–2 should establish facts. Re-run the open model on a representative, blinded set of real contract types, including difficult clauses, customer-specific playbooks, edge cases, latency, privacy, and failure modes—not just the headline benchmark. Model total serving cost, migration cost, human-review cost, licensing, security, data-residency, and reliability. Interview customers and analyze renewals and losses to quantify which parts of the workflow drive purchase, trust, and expansion. Map which competitors could combine the model with credible workflow or distribution.

Weeks 3–4 should produce a controlled pilot behind an abstraction layer so the company can compare or route between the existing extractor and the open model without locking the product to either one. Test quality by segment, safe fallback, observability, and the effect of errors on approvals and audit trails. Begin security, licensing, compliance, and legal review. Keep the incumbent model available until the replacement meets explicit thresholds.

Weeks 5–6 should deepen the parts that remain hard to copy: faster capture and maintenance of company playbooks, correct routing of exceptions, richer integrations into procurement and approval systems, defensible audit evidence, and feedback that improves each customer's workflow. Product and sales should describe the value in terms of controlled contract decisions and organizational memory, not proprietary extraction. Do not claim the model itself as a moat.

Weeks 7–8 should roll the new extractor to a limited production cohort if it clears quality, reliability, compliance, and cost gates. Use the savings where they improve the business most—better margins, lower entry friction, or investment in workflow—rather than automatically starting a price war. Maintain dual-sourcing or an exit path where feasible.

At the end of eight weeks, the board should receive a build-versus-adopt decision backed by real economics, a model-agnostic architecture plan, customer evidence about the durable buying criteria, and explicit transition triggers. Reconsider model training only if the company identifies a proprietary data advantage and a customer-critical performance gap that external models cannot economically close. Escalate the threat classification if the model or a new entrant begins delivering the full governed workflow, integrations, and trust layer; benchmark parity in a single step is not enough.
