# Raw evaluation output
Case ID: scenario-01
Reviewer ID: business-model-baseline-001

The company has strong evidence of a useful product and a promising problem-solution fit, but it does not yet have a validated business model. Retention, engagement, and avoided payroll failures show that users receive recurring value. They do not establish who has budget, who makes a purchase decision, what that person will pay, or whether the best revenue model is owner-paid software, an accountant channel, a lender partnership, or something else. Advertising now would optimize a revenue mechanism before the buyer and value exchange are understood. It could also weaken trust in a product handling sensitive cash-flow data.

The next six weeks should be treated as a buyer and willingness-to-pay discovery sprint, not as a pricing-page launch.

In week 1, segment the existing user base using observable characteristics such as restaurant size, payroll frequency, forecast usage, accountant involvement, and severity of prevented cash shortfalls. Recruit a balanced group of retained users, recent churned users, accountants serving those restaurants, and relevant lenders. Instrument the product so the team can connect forecast use and payroll-risk events to each segment rather than relying on aggregate engagement.

In weeks 1 and 2, run structured interviews about the most recent payroll-risk incident: what happened, what the app changed, what the alternative would have cost, who noticed the result, who controls the relevant budget, and how software of this kind is approved. Avoid asking only whether someone “would pay,” because a favorable answer carries little commitment. Interviews should produce several concrete buyer hypotheses, for example an owner buying risk reduction, an accountant buying a multi-client monitoring tool, or a lender subsidizing better borrower visibility.

In weeks 2 through 4, put those hypotheses behind real offers. Show restaurant owners two or three annual or monthly packages tied to the recurring forecasting outcome and ask for a credit card, refundable deposit, or signed paid-pilot agreement. Offer accountants a multi-client dashboard pilot with a specific price and start date. Explore a lender pilot only if the data-sharing, consent, and incentive issues can be made explicit. Randomize or sequence price points across comparable prospects instead of presenting a single arbitrary price. The commitment should be meaningful enough to distinguish purchasing behavior from enthusiasm.

In weeks 4 and 5, deliver the smallest paid experience necessary to learn. Track conversion by buyer type and segment, sales effort, objections, time to decision, continued product use, and any support or data requirements. Interview people who decline as carefully as those who buy. Keep the free product stable during the experiment so that a sudden paywall does not confound willingness to pay with surprise or loss aversion.

In week 6, compare the candidate models on committed revenue, repeatability, acquisition access, gross-margin implications, trust, and operational complexity. A credible next step would require more than clicks on a pricing page: it should show that a defined buyer accepts a defined offer at a price that can support the required sales and service motion. If restaurant owners commit most consistently, expand that paid test. If accountants produce higher conversion and lower acquisition cost, develop the channel or multi-client product. If no segment makes a real commitment, continue monetization discovery rather than covering the uncertainty with ads. Ads can remain a later hypothesis, but only after the team has compared their economics and trust costs with models connected directly to the payroll value already demonstrated.
