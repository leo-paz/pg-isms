# Raw evaluation output
Case ID: scenario-05
Reviewer ID: business-model-baseline-001

This is currently a profitable, software-enabled specialist services company, and that can be a deliberate venture type rather than a temporary failure to become a software startup. The evidence supports recurring client demand, healthy operations, and a tool that improves delivery productivity. It does not yet show that clients want the tool without the founders' judgment, implementation, and accountability. The reachable $14 million market and the founders' preference for durable distributions also do not naturally match the return profile and scale expectations of conventional venture capital.

The company boundary should preserve the parts clients are demonstrably buying. Keep regulatory judgment, implementation, client accountability, and the client relationship inside the company. Keep developing the internal tool where it improves consistency, capacity, turnaround time, and margins. Standardize repeatable work into productized service packages, documentation, and workflows so growth is less dependent on bespoke founder labor. Commodity activities that do not create trust, learning, or quality advantage can be outsourced selectively, with review and accountability retained internally.

The founders should not eliminate services merely to make the company look like a pure software business. They can test whether a separate software product exists without betting the whole firm on it. For example, offer a paid client-facing module or limited license to a small set of existing and new customers, require an actual contract, and measure implementation burden, independent usage, renewal intent, support cost, and whether buyers still require advisory work. If those tests reveal a distinct buyer, repeatable onboarding, attractive software margins, and a larger market beyond the current specialist niche, the founders can reconsider a product subsidiary or a more software-centric model. Until then, the tool is best understood as proprietary operational leverage within the service business.

The appropriate capital model is primarily retained earnings and customer-funded growth. The company has positive cash flow and no acute runway problem, so equity financing would impose dilution, governance obligations, and growth expectations without solving a demonstrated financing constraint. The founders can set a reinvestment policy that funds tool development and measured hiring while maintaining their desired distributions. A credit line or modest term debt could be useful for predictable working-capital needs or an investment with a clear repayment path, but it is not required by the facts.

This choice should be revisited if the opportunity changes. A much larger independently validated software market, a time-sensitive land grab, or a capital-intensive distribution advantage could justify outside equity. Conversely, if the founders continue to prefer income, control, and a bounded specialist market, a focused profitable company may be the better design. The decision should follow the customers, market size, operating model, and founders' goals—not the accelerator's assumption that every software-enabled firm must pursue venture scale.
