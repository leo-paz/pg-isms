# Raw evaluation output
Case ID: scenario-02
Reviewer ID: business-model-baseline-001

The immediate problem is supply resilience and delivery reliability, not proof that the company must own production. Calling a component “core” does not by itself make vertical integration sensible. The company needs control over module availability, quality, interchangeability, and technical knowledge. It may be able to obtain that control contractually and architecturally without taking on a $2.4 million factory, a twelve-month delay, and a capability the current team lacks.

For the next 90 days, I would make dual sourcing the primary path, preserve vertical integration as an option to be evaluated, and decide on the standard interface using evidence gathered during qualification.

During days 1-15, appoint one owner for the decision and build a full comparison of the alternatives. The comparison should include purchase price, rework, delay penalties, expedited freight, engineering supervision, working capital, lost installations, customer consequences, and concentration risk—not merely $180 versus $230. The team should also map which knowledge, tooling rights, calibration data, test procedures, and quality records are controlled by the incumbent. Counsel and operations should review the current contract for tooling ownership, service levels, audit rights, capacity commitments, and transition rights.

At the same time, begin the second supplier's six-week qualification program. A $50 unit premium is inexpensive insurance if it prevents missed installations, but qualification must test actual yield, calibration stability, throughput, traceability, and delivery performance. Place a limited pilot order and agree in writing that qualification creates no exclusivity. Negotiate a near-term recovery plan with the incumbent: weekly production visibility, quality targets, priority capacity, root-cause work on the 7% rework, and remedies or escalation for missed commitments. These actions create real alternatives while preserving supply.

During days 15-45, specify the standard interface and estimate its total impact. Ten engineering weeks and $70,000 may be justified if the interface reduces switching cost for future suppliers, lowers redesign work, and avoids proprietary lock-in across a meaningful production volume. The design review should determine whether standardization affects validation, sensor performance, warranty exposure, or the product roadmap. If engineering work can begin without disrupting critical customer commitments, start the reversible portions while the supplier qualification is running.

By approximately day 45, the team should have qualification results and better cost-of-delay data. If the second supplier passes, allocate a controlled share of new orders to it and measure both suppliers against the same acceptance and on-time-delivery scorecard. Use volume commitments only in exchange for measurable reliability, pricing, capacity reservation, or rights to necessary tooling and documentation. Do not grant exclusivity.

During days 45-75, complete a make-versus-buy model for internal production. It should include hiring and training, management attention, ramp yield, certification, maintenance, facilities, working capital, opportunity cost of the $2.4 million, and the fact that an internal line does not help the next two quarters. Identify intermediate options as well, such as acquiring tooling rights, funding a supplier-held duplicate tool, contracting a specialist manufacturer, or hiring a manufacturing engineer to supervise external partners. The board should see the conditions under which ownership becomes attractive—for example very high stable volume, strategically unique process learning, unacceptable supplier margins, or an inability to secure capacity and IP by contract.

By day 90, choose a boundary deliberately. On the present facts, the likely decision is to keep module fabrication outside the company, qualify the second supplier, adopt the standard interface if its validation and opportunity costs are acceptable, and retain calibration specifications, acceptance testing, system design, and supplier-quality control internally. Vertical integration should advance only if the 90-day work uncovers a durable advantage that outweighs the capital, time, and capability gap. This gives the company near-term resilience and long-term bargaining power without pretending that a twelve-month factory is an answer to an eight-week delay.
