# Raw evaluation output
Case ID: scenario-03
Reviewer ID: business-model-baseline-001

The current flat $18,000 price ignores large differences in customer value, usage, and cost to serve. However, the available evidence does not support copying a competitor's menu. A $29-per-seat self-serve tier is especially mismatched to an enterprise compliance purchase with long procurement and large avoided-remediation value. An unlimited plan would also hide the fact that four accounts cause 68% of compute and support cost.

The renewal decision should start with account-level economics. For all 24 customers, assemble actual usage, support hours, compute cost, procurement effort, audit frequency, number of business units or facilities covered, realized outcome, renewal history, and likely expansion. Interview the economic buyer and operational champion separately. The goal is to learn which measurable dimension most closely follows value and which follows cost. Seats may be neither: a small compliance team can protect a very large operation, while workload may depend on audit volume, documents, facilities, integrations, or analysis runs.

I would test a three-part packaging structure before the next renewal cycle:

1. A focused archive or readiness package with clear limits on active audits, integrations, support, and compute. It should still carry an enterprise minimum that pays for procurement and account setup; it should not be presented as low-priced self-serve unless customers can actually discover, buy, implement, and support it without sales involvement.
2. A core audit-operations package for regular users, including an appropriate usage allowance and standard support. Its price should reflect the six-figure remediation exposure being reduced, not just current internal cost.
3. A complex or high-scale package for accounts needing greater usage, multiple entities, integrations, premium response times, or intensive advisory support. Price it as a platform fee plus a transparent capacity or usage component, or with bounded tiers. Do not make it unlimited.

Exact prices should be established through renewal conversations, not guessed from the data provided. Prepare account-specific proposals at several plausible price levels and conduct them early enough to observe real negotiation behavior. For the twelve weekly users with documented $120,000-$400,000 avoided costs, test materially higher annual contracts while connecting the increase to coverage, support, and business outcome. For the four expensive accounts, calculate a price floor that protects gross margin and present a high-scale package or paid overage. For the low-use archive accounts, offer a narrower package at a sensible enterprise minimum; accept that some may leave if they do not value even that offer. Do not use the smallest accounts' low ongoing support to ignore their high acquisition and procurement cost.

The sales motion should remain sales-assisted for the coming cycle. There are only 24 annual customers, the value is consequential, and procurement is nontrivial. Standardize qualification, demos, proposals, and onboarding so the motion becomes more efficient, but do not declare it self-serve merely because a low sticker price is published. A genuinely self-serve offer can be tested separately with new prospects only after the team can deliver a short buying and onboarding path without human intervention.

Manage the transition explicitly. Give customers advance notice, preserve contract commitments, and avoid permanent grandfathering that recreates the same economics. Where an increase is large, a one-cycle ramp can reduce shock in exchange for a longer commitment or a case study. Track renewal, expansion, gross margin, sales time, objections, and downgrade behavior by package. The decision is successful if the new structure makes value and service boundaries clearer, improves unit economics, and preserves the customers for whom the product is mission-critical—not if it merely resembles a competitor's pricing page.
