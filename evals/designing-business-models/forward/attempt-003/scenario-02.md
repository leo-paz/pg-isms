## Boundary diagnosis

This is a hardware company with a validated customer product and a supplier dependency that is already harming delivery. The current supplier costs $180 per module but causes eight-week delays, 7% rework, and missed installations. It owns specialized tooling. An internal line costs at least $2.4 million, arrives no sooner than an estimated twelve months, and requires capabilities the 14-person company lacks. Calling the module “core” does not show that ownership is economical or executable.

The company should retain ownership of the calibration specification, acceptance data, customer relationship, validation record, and interface standard. It should determine whether tooling rights, test fixtures, failure data, and switching assistance are available from the incumbent; those are current facts to verify. The company should not assume a supplier defect when transport, incoming inspection, integration, installation mix, or specification changes could confound the 7% rework result.

Immediate integration cannot cure the next 90 days of delay and exposes $2.4 million plus unknown operating, hiring, yield-ramp, certification, working-capital, and management costs. The simple $50 unit-price premium for the second supplier would require 48,000 modules to equal $2.4 million before any internal operating cost; that comparison still omits the value of avoided delays and rework. The decision must use total coordination and dependency cost, not nominal piece price.

## 90-day decision

**Days 0–14: establish the failure ledger.** The operations lead owns a lot-level record of quoted and actual lead time, calibration yield, rework cause and cost, missed-install contribution margin, customer penalties or churn risk, expedite cost, engineering attention, and working-capital exposure. Engineering audits the specification and acceptance process. Commercial leadership records customer effects. The company simultaneously negotiates an incumbent recovery plan with service levels, failure-data access, tooling/switching terms, and dated corrective actions.

**Days 1–42: qualify the second supplier.** Spend the disclosed six weeks on a representative qualification lot at $230 per module. Use the same blinded acceptance protocol and enough units to exercise expected variation; the exact sample size is an engineering assumption to set before testing. Measure calibration drift, first-pass yield, rework, lead-time reliability, physical reliability, validation effort, and support responsiveness. No exclusivity is an advantage because it preserves replacement.

**Days 1–70: build the switching path.** Authorize the $70,000, ten-engineering-week standard-interface effort behind two guards: no compromise to validated performance, and a capped engineering schedule that does not endanger customer installations. Clarify whether “ten engineering weeks” means elapsed or person-weeks before authorization. The company owns the interface and conformance suite; both suppliers must pass it. This creates a dual-source option instead of exchanging one lock-in for another.

**Days 43–89: run controlled production lots.** If supplier two passes qualification, allocate a bounded share of new modules to it while retaining the incumbent during recovery. Compare on-time installations and total delivered cost, including rework and coordination. Do not change both supplier and installation process in the same comparison. Product and lot mix, shipping conditions, and customer site complexity are predeclared confounders.

Review at days 14, 42, 70, and 90. The COO owns the experiment; engineering owns conformance; finance validates total-cost and cash exposure. The bounded exposure is $70,000, ten engineering weeks, qualification cost, and a limited second-source production lot—not $2.4 million.

Precommit the boundary branches:

- **dual-source:** Supplier two passes validation and delivers materially better lead-time reliability or total delivered cost, and the interface passes without product regression. Split volume with performance-based allocations and maintain a replacement path.
- **partner:** The incumbent's corrective action restores acceptable yield and lead time, but keep the qualified alternate and interface to prevent renewed dependency.
- **repair:** Root cause lies mainly in the company's specification, interface, incoming inspection, transport, or installation process. Repair that architecture/process before attributing failure to the supplier.
- **integrate:** Consider an internal line only if measured annual failure and dependency costs, customer-capture risk, and strategic control benefits exceed the full capital, delay, capability, operating, certification, and management burden; a credible hiring/ramp plan and financing gate must also exist.
- **stop:** Stop a supplier or interface path that fails validation, worsens reliability, breaches customer commitments, or exceeds its capped exposure.

The selected 90-day branch is **repair plus qualify, interface, and dual-source if the qualification passes**. Reject immediate vertical integration on current evidence, while preserving it as a later option. Delay now costs missed installations; internalization adds at least twelve months and cannot address that cost. At day 90, business models selects the partner or dual-source boundary. Engineering then owns the enduring interface, while any future integration proposal must return with the full capability and capital gate.
