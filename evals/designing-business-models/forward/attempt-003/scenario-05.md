## Venture-type decision

Select a **durable, software-enabled specialist company now**, while preserving a bounded venture-product option. This is not a claim that consultancy is inherently better. It follows the present evidence: $1.6 million annual revenue, about $352,000 operating profit at a 22% margin, 90% renewal, no survival constraint, a reachable specialist market estimated at $14 million, and founders who prefer durable cash distributions. Clients still buy judgment, implementation, and accountability. The internal tool's 35% delivery-time improvement proves leverage inside the service; it does not yet prove that service is merely scaffolding or that customers will buy a standalone product.

Compare the two paths explicitly:

- **Durable path:** The observed market is $14 million annually, current revenue is $1.6 million, operating margin is 22%, renewal is 90%, and customer funding already works. Growth can be paced, hiring can follow profitable demand, evidence arrives through paid engagements and renewals, and the founder distribution goal fits. Downside includes a specialist-market ceiling, founder/key-person dependence, and potentially service-heavy delivery. Plausible outcomes include long-term distributions or a sale to a services, compliance, or software buyer; the actual buyer set is a fact to verify.
- **Venture-product path:** Reachable product market, standalone price, product gross margin, repeatable acquisition, implementation burden, expansion, required hiring, capital absorption, and plausible venture-scale exits are all unknown. Its upside could exceed the specialist market if the tool transfers across customers without bespoke judgment. Its downside is sacrificing a profitable company and founder goals before that transfer is proven. Evidence latency is at least a paid deployment and renewal cycle, not an accelerator assertion.

The accelerator's prescribed billion-dollar outcome is therefore rejected as a current branch. Equity should accelerate an evidenced venture-return path, not manufacture one.

## Company boundary

Keep regulatory judgment, final accountability, client relationships, and the internal tool/standard under company control. Assume client data remains client-controlled and verify contractual permission, security, retention, and audit requirements before any product test. Productize repeatable ingestion, workflow, validation, and report-generation modules behind a stable interface. Partner only for replaceable, well-specified work with service levels and a switching path; do not outsource the judgment or accountability clients are demonstrably buying.

Do not eliminate services. Instead, measure whether implementation and judgment fall as the product standardizes. Supplier failure, product-interface failure, and client-specific regulatory complexity must be attributed separately. The boundary changes only when reduced coordination and repeatability benefits exceed the capability, quality, and customer-capture cost.

## Bounded product/repeatability test

Over 120 days, offer ten qualified organizations a standardized reporting platform with a defined implementation cap and no bespoke feature work. A $24,000–$48,000 annual license plus a separately priced fixed onboarding package is a labeled test assumption, not a settled price. Include existing clients and qualified nonclients so founder relationships do not fully confound demand. The business-model lead owns the test; product owns the deployment; total engineering and delivery investment is capped in advance and funded from operations.

Acceptance requires a signed annual order, cleared payment, authorized data, and live production use. Track sales source and time, deployment hours, share of work needing senior regulatory judgment, report accuracy, client accountability expectations, support cost, gross margin, usage, renewal intent, and effects on the existing 90% renewal base. Praise, accelerator interest, free pilots, and a buyer requesting custom consulting do not count as product acceptance. Confounders include regulatory complexity, client data quality, founder-led sales, and work shifted into unpriced onboarding.

Set the following assumptions before kickoff and do not move them after seeing results: at least five of ten paid deployments at the target band; no more than 20 hours of standardized onboarding per deployment; at least 70% product gross margin after support and compute; and a majority of recurring workflow completed without bespoke founder judgment. Also require credible evidence that the product's reachable market extends materially beyond the $14 million specialist market. Review on day 60 and make the branch decision on day 120.

Precommit the branches:

- **durable:** If customers continue to pay mainly for judgment, implementation, or accountability, keep the consultancy boundary and use the tool to improve margin, capacity, and distributions.
- **dual-track:** If one segment buys and deploys the standardized package but attached expertise remains material, maintain a capped product line alongside services and gather renewal evidence before changing the whole company.
- **venture-product:** Switch only if paid deployments, repeatability, margin, market reach, acquisition evidence, and founder consent jointly support venture returns. Then define the hiring and capital-absorption plan before raising equity.
- **repair:** If failures come from the interface or onboarding rather than the underlying value, make one bounded repair and retest.
- **stop:** Stop standalone product investment if it harms regulatory quality, client accountability, renewal, profitability, or exceeds the cap.

The selected capital model is customer funding and retained earnings. Reject immediate venture capital, a 30-person hiring plan, and service elimination. Preserve the equity option until the day-120 milestone and subsequent paid retention evidence. The switch condition is not “software exists”; it is a repeatable, paid, sufficiently large product path that the founders actually want. Until then, the delay cost of not raising is low, while premature equity and organizational expansion would be difficult to reverse.
