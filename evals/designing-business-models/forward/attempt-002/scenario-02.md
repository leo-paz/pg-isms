## Boundary diagnosis

The module is important, but “core” is not evidence for ownership. The current supplier creates eight-week delay, 7% rework, and missed installations. Yet immediate integration would commit $2.4 million, roughly twelve months, and scarce management attention while requiring capabilities the 14-person company does not have. It would not solve the next 90 days. A qualified alternative costs $50 more per module, is nonexclusive, and can be tested in six weeks; a $70,000 standard interface and ten engineering weeks could make both suppliers replaceable. The reversible supplier and interface paths should be tested before irreversible line construction.

This is validated hardware, so the record must include inventory and working-capital effects, tooling rights, fabrication and calibration process control, certification impact, reliability, field failure, rework, supplier qualification, and current component economics. Verify during days 1–10: annual and forecast module volume; contribution and customer loss per delayed installation; cash available; failure-mode attribution; incumbent tooling/IP and transfer rights; qualification and certification requirements; lead-time and yield history by lot; warranty exposure; second-source capacity; and the true all-in internal-line cost, ramp yield, management load, and time to validated output.

## Boundary record

- **Incumbent-only/repair:** $180 per module and no new capability, but unacceptable dependency, delay, rework, and customer-capture risk unless enforceable service and quality terms change.
- **Second-source/dual-source:** $230 per module plus a six-week qualification. It raises variable cost by $50 but buys near-term replaceability and evidence without exclusivity. Capacity, quality, and certification remain to be proven.
- **Standard interface:** $70,000 and ten engineering weeks. It reduces switching and coordination cost if both suppliers can meet one controlled specification. Confirm that the work does not require unsafe certification shortcuts or delay more valuable product commitments.
- **Internal line:** customer, quality, and schedule control could improve, but it adds $2.4 million of fixed capital, twelve months of delay, tooling and working capital, and a new manufacturing/calibration capability. It remains an option, not the current branch.

Keep customer contracts, calibration data, acceptance standards, and the supplier interface under company control. Suppliers may own specialized production assets only where the company has documented data portability, tooling/escrow terms where feasible, service levels, audit rights, and a tested switching path. Attribute failures by common incoming inspection and field telemetry so an interface defect is not mistaken for a supplier defect, or vice versa.

## Ninety-day boundary test

The COO owns one bounded dual-source-and-interface test, with the quality lead accountable for validation and engineering accountable for the interface. Authorize the known $70,000 interface spend, ten engineering weeks, the modules needed for a qualification lot and limited field pilot, and no internal-line capex. The board reviews at days 45 and 90.

1. **Days 1–10:** freeze the validated performance and data interface; obtain the incumbent's corrective-action plan and proposed lead-time, yield, warranty, and service commitments; contract the second supplier without exclusivity; order a qualification lot; and establish common incoming, calibration, environmental, reliability, and field tests.
2. **Days 11–45:** run the six-week second-source qualification while engineering implements the standard interface behind a reversible configuration. Instrument each failure to separate module, interface, installation, and system causes. Do not expose customers before the same safety, reliability, and certification gates as the incumbent module are passed.
3. **Days 46–75:** if qualification passes, deploy a capped pilot across representative installations and compare on-time delivery, first-pass yield, rework, calibration drift, field reliability, install time, support, and working-capital days. Continue a matched incumbent lot.
4. **Days 76–90:** calculate total landed cost, including the $50 unit premium, engineering, rework, delay, support, inventory buffer, and missed-installation cost. Negotiate nonexclusive volume allocations and service terms, then execute the precommitted branch.

Acceptance requires the second source to pass the existing validated specification and all required certification/safety gates, then meet the predeclared operating assumptions of at least 95% on-time delivery and no more than 2% rework in the bounded pilot, with no material reliability regression. Those two numbers are decision thresholds, not claims about current industry norms. A supplier “pass” based only on samples, promises, or a lower quote is rejection. Confounders include lot size, installation crew, expedited freight, interface changes, seasonality, and component substitutions.

## Precommitted decision

- `dual-source`: if the second source passes and total expected delay/rework reduction exceeds its $50 unit premium plus qualification/interface cost, allocate enough nonexclusive volume to keep both sources qualified and test switching periodically.
- `partner`: if one supplier clearly dominates but remains replaceable through the interface and acceptable terms, use that specialist without granting exclusivity.
- `repair`: if the incumbent meets a dated corrective-action and service threshold while the second source fails for supplier-specific reasons, retain it temporarily and continue qualifying alternatives.
- `integrate`: reconsider the internal line only if both external paths fail or quantified recurring dependency/control losses exceed the annualized $2.4 million, ramp, delay, capability, management, and working-capital costs—and the company can fund and staff the capability without endangering deliveries.
- `stop`: stop any source or interface release that fails calibration, reliability, certification, safety, or truthful customer commitments.

The selected 90-day branch is qualification plus a standard switching interface, not immediate integration. It preserves the internal-line option while addressing the current dependency much sooner. The cost of delaying integration is another 90 days of possible supplier pain, bounded with inventory and customer scheduling guards; the cost of integrating now is a twelve-month, capability-heavy commitment without comparative evidence. At day 90, business models chooses the boundary branch; engineering then owns the interface and operations owns the qualified supply plan.
