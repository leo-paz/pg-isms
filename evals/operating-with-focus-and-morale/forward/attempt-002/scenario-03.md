## One state-changing priority

**Outcome:** By August 12, restore a predictable release-and-customer-learning loop by transferring routine release authority from the CEO to the engineering DRIs. Across the four-week cycle, at least 90% of routine release decisions must be made within four business hours without CEO action; no scheduled user call may be skipped for conference or internal work; there may be no post-implementation CEO reversal unless genuinely new security, legal, safety, contractual, or material customer evidence appears; and there may be no rollback attributable to a late approval or avoidable late change. Total production rollbacks are limited to one.

This outcome can change the company state faster and more credibly than a conference launch: it restores decision velocity, customer evidence, and service reliability for 70 paying customers while testing the suspected bottleneck.

- **Baseline:** Last month: three post-implementation reversals, three rollbacks, nine missed user calls, and routine decisions waiting into the night. The exact approval-latency denominator is established July 16.
- **Accountable owner:** CEO for installing the system; Release DRI for daily release outcomes; Customer Continuity DRI for calls. The CEO may not reclaim a delegated decision after implementation merely because she would have chosen differently.
- **Affected users and work:** All 70 paying customers, the nine missed-call customers, the two engineering leads, and engineers shipping production changes.
- **Proposed mechanism:** Sustained overload plus centralized, late authority is creating delayed decisions, rework, rollbacks, and displaced user learning. Clear decision rights, recovery, smaller releases, and direct user evidence should improve judgment and throughput.
- **Causal rival:** The dominant cause could instead be weak release engineering/QA or an unclear product strategy. The July 18 incident review and four weekly release cycles discriminate among these explanations.
- **Evidence latency:** Approval latency and skipped calls can be read honestly after one week, on July 22. Rollback and customer-learning evidence needs the full four-week cycle, with weekly checks.
- **Falsifier:** If two consecutive weekly cycles with explicit authority, smaller staged changes, and workload safeguards still produce routine approval waits over four business hours, repeated late reversals, or rollbacks from strategic ambiguity, delegation alone is not the sufficient tactic. The CEO must adapt the decision architecture and product scope; she must not compensate with nights.
- **Cycle end:** August 12, or immediately on an acute health/safety event, P0 customer-continuity event, legal/security boundary, or newly verified survival threat.
- **Essential obligations preserved:** Production support and incident response, security/privacy, payroll and fiduciary work, contractual commitments, timely teammate coordination, customer support and the nine make-up calls, and qualified health care.

## Protected execution and coverage

1. **Authority before calendar changes.** By July 16 the CEO publishes a one-page decision-rights record. Release DRI owns normal reversible releases within existing product and risk guardrails. Engineering Lead 2 acts if the DRI is unavailable and acknowledges escalations within two business hours. CEO escalation is limited to breaking API changes, material contractual or pricing commitments, legal/security/privacy/safety risk, and genuinely irreversible company-level decisions. Those items use a written brief with recommendation, evidence, deadline, and fallback—not an open-ended late-night queue.

2. **Engineering maker time.** Monday-Thursday, 9:00 a.m.-noon local time is meeting- and notification-free for engineering, except an active incident or qualified safety/security/legal issue. Conference work gets no access to these blocks. Releases use small changes, staged exposure, named rollback owners, and a written decision log.

3. **CEO work mix.** The CEO is not assigned a fictitious maker-heavy calendar. Her protected blocks are Tuesday and Thursday, 9:00-11:00 a.m., for product/customer synthesis and maintaining the decision system; meetings are concentrated from 1:00-4:00 p.m. She attends at least two customer calls per week but does not become the call-scheduling bottleneck.

4. **Direct user contact.** The Customer Continuity DRI rebooks all nine missed calls on July 16, completes at least six by July 22 and all nine by July 29, then protects at least three customer calls per week through August 12. A customer-facing teammate covers any CEO absence and records notes, blocker, promised follow-up, owner, and due date in the shared CRM.

5. **Availability and escalation.** There are no routine night approvals. The CEO is unavailable from 8:00 p.m.-8:00 a.m. and on two consecutive recovery days each week. The Release DRI and Engineering Lead 2 cover ordinary decisions. The existing incident commander handles P0 incidents; security/privacy matters go to the security owner and counsel as appropriate. Every handoff lives in the shared decision and incident log. Deep-work blocks never override active customer, teammate, incident, safety, legal, or continuity needs.

6. **Workload and recovery safeguards.** The CEO stops work after the July 15 handoff, takes two consecutive fully offline days by July 20, and works no more than 45 hours in any of the next four weeks, with no routine nights. The rest of the team receives the same no-compulsory-nights rule and at least two days off weekly. This is a safety ceiling, not a determination score or a quota to fill. The exceptional-push end date is July 15; no new exceptional push is authorized in this cycle.

7. **Customer continuity.** Conference scope is cut before support coverage or user calls are cut. Any unresolved production blocker, missed contractual response, or renewal risk can displace conference work. The Customer Continuity DRI owns that call, with CEO escalation only for a material commercial commitment.

## Morale and persistence

There is no direct observation that “morale is low,” so the company should not diagnose motivation by fiat. The observed signals are loss of agency (two leads waiting), invisible or reversed progress (three reversals), service instability (three rollbacks), lost user contact (nine calls), and prolonged nights. The leading mechanisms to test are centralized authority and degraded judgment from unsustainable workload; QA weakness and strategy ambiguity remain rivals.

Morale repair is operational: give the leads real decision ownership, make small reliable releases visible, reconnect the team with customer evidence, keep the four-week finish line honest, provide recovery, and give the CEO a trusted private board/adviser check-in. Accountability stays private and tied to decisions and outcomes; there is no public hour tally or conference-pressure ritual.

- **Goal premise:** Paying customers will benefit when the company reliably improves the API based on direct customer evidence.
- **Current tactic:** Clear delegated release authority, smaller staged changes, protected customer contact, and a minimal evidence-gated conference presence.
- **Tactic being stopped:** A CEO-centralized, conference-timed, four-week hours push.
- **Hard phase versus tactic failure:** A difficult month or a competitor/conference deadline does not invalidate the product goal. The recent reversals, rollbacks, skipped calls, and approval waits are already enough to reject the current hours-and-centralization tactic.
- **Premise failure:** Reopen the goal only after two consecutive bounded weekly tests of delegated/smaller releases and renewed customer contact still fail, and customer evidence shows the selected work does not solve a meaningful problem—or sooner if survival, material harm, or a superior opportunity changes the objective. Prior investment and effort are not proof of validity.
