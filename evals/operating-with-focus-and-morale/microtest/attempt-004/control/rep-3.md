## Test and renewal plan

The 61% to 79% attendance change is a promising observation, not proof: it had no control, lasted two weeks, and may reflect the conductor. The lapsed-member calls point toward unclear progress, but do not establish causality or renewal effect. Fatigue and scheduling remain plausible contributors.

**July 15–17 — establish the baseline.** Rowan freezes definitions and creates a member-level sheet with choir, conductor, attendance for the last six rehearsals, renewal status/date, and—only when volunteered—reason. Ellis records conductor schedule and any recent rehearsal changes. Flag the 52 low-attendance members for outreach, but do not label them churned. Rowan sends a short, non-pressuring check-in asking which factor most affects attendance: schedule, perceived progress, price, fatigue/energy, or another reason. Existing call evidence stays separate from new responses.

**July 18–August 7 — run a repeatable trial.** Ellis randomly assigns two choirs to use a standardized written song goal at the start plus a simple closing rehearsal recording; two continue normal practice. If random assignment is operationally impossible, document the assignment reason before results are seen. All conductors receive the same one-page instructions; no other intentional rehearsal-format changes occur during the trial. Record rehearsal attendance, whether the tactic was delivered, cancellations, substitutes, and conductor. The recording is for members under existing consent/privacy practice; if that practice does not cover it, use an in-room playback or written progress recap instead.

This is a small operational test, not definitive research. Compare change from each choir's prior six-rehearsal attendance baseline, treatment versus comparison, and low-attendance versus other members. Also ask one identical weekly question: “This week, could you tell whether you were improving?” Keep conductor-level results visible so a conductor effect is not hidden.

**July 24 checkpoint.** Ellis and Rowan inspect delivery, missing data, workload, member complaints, and attendance—not renewal conclusions. Continue if the tactic was delivered consistently, creates no material privacy/member problem, and adds no more than 15 minutes of conductor work per rehearsal. Otherwise simplify once or stop it; record why. Do not expand based on an early positive number.

**August 7 checkpoint.** Ellis and Rowan publish a one-page readout: cohort sizes, baseline and trial attendance, progress-response rates, delivery fidelity, workload, complaints, and limitations. Branch:

- If both treatment choirs improve relative to comparison, the effect is not concentrated in one conductor, and workload/complaints remain acceptable, Ellis adopts the practice across all four choirs for renewal season.
- If results are mixed or conductor-specific, continue only in the promising choir(s) and describe it as an experiment.
- If there is no improvement, delivery is inconsistent, or burden/complaints are material, stop it. Rowan then bases renewal messaging on existing program value without claiming measured progress.

**August 8–9 — renewal readiness.** Rowan verifies every member has correct price, dates, cancellation terms, rehearsal schedule, and a working renewal path. Test the flow internally. Prepare segmented outreach for all members, with personal follow-up to the 52 low-attendance members. **August 10 — renewal opens.** Rowan sends the factual renewal message. Track daily: eligible members, renewed, declined, undecided/no response, delivery failures, questions older than 24 hours, and stated reasons. Report by choir and prior-attendance band, not just the aggregate.

**August 14 checkpoint.** Rowan checks contact failures and the 24-hour reply standard. If replies breach the standard or errors recur, pause all nonessential showcase preparation for the responsible staff and add temporary coverage. Do not launch a new marketing project to compensate.
