# Decision memo

**To:** CanopyTrace leadership  
**Date:** May 11, 2026  
**Subject:** Focus the next five weeks on a comparable battery trial and sustainable service

## Recommendation

Keep the 90-day-cycle objective as the single company priority through June 15, but do not declare a technical winner yet. Pause the four unrelated roadmap efforts and stop actively advancing three incomparable battery approaches at once. Run one controlled, representative field experiment centered on the sleep-mode profile, with the current profile as its control, while doing only bounded desk work on the new cell and solar option. This is not an endorsement of sleep mode: it is the approach with enough near-term field availability to produce useful evidence within five weeks.

Protect current growers by maintaining contracted support and ordinary response coverage. At the same time, remove planned weekend checks and engineering overtime. Leadership should treat capacity, service quality, and team pulse as operating constraints, not secondary concerns to revisit after the battery problem.

## Why this is the right move now

The company has a real gap against its objective: the median first change is day 57, with 94 requested visits across the last two cycles. But the business impact is unknown. No crop loss or missed response window shows that service has contained the problem; it does not show that visits are harmless to renewal, expansion, grower labor, or CanopyTrace's support capacity.

None of the three results proves a solution. The cell's 108 lab days omit field performance, certification, lead-time, and unit-economics questions. Sleep mode's projected gain is encouraging, but alert latency increased materially. Solar worked in two sunny sites and failed frequently in shade. Different revisions and reporting intervals prevent a fair comparison.

## Operating plan through June 15

Appoint one accountable experiment lead under the existing written decision rights. By May 15, that lead should publish a short protocol with a fixed sensor revision, reporting interval, starting battery condition, placement categories, and measurement definitions. Use a representative subset of growers and greenhouse conditions; do not disrupt certified irrigation or alarm systems. Randomize or otherwise balance current-profile controls against sleep-mode devices. Record battery trajectory, low-battery events, alert-delivery latency, requested and preventive visits, support time, and any customer-visible degradation.

In parallel, assign tightly limited work to resolve questions that do not require full development: obtain the new cell's certification scope, landed cost assumptions, availability, and earliest realistic field-test date; document where solar is technically viable and where shade makes it unsuitable. These are decision inputs, not active product tracks.

The product lead should conduct structured conversations with current growers and quantify visit burden: grower labor, disruption, perceived reliability, renewal intent, and expansion implications. Distinguish absence of crop loss from willingness to tolerate visits. Do not promise a particular solution or infer commercial impact from anecdotes.

Replan work to each person's normal schedule, with no planned nights or weekends. Cancel nonessential trial checks rather than squeezing them into overtime. Cross-train a designated backup for the field specialist and publish a coverage rota that preserves contracted response. Engineering should support field visits only through planned rotation. Review weekly: service response, trial integrity, hours, visit load, and the team pulse. If the plan exceeds available capacity, reduce trial scope before increasing hours.

## Do not do in the next five weeks

Do not restart the enclosure refresh, analytics screen, small-farm kit, or reseller packaging. Do not place a production cell order, begin unscoped certification, expand solar deployment, or roll sleep mode across the fleet. Do not use the board date to manufacture certainty, treat 108 lab days as field proof, or interpret zero crop loss as proof of zero customer cost. Avoid further engineer reassignments except for a customer-continuity issue.

## Decisions for June 15

Leadership should decide whether the 90-day objective remains the highest-value objective after reviewing quantified customer burden, service cost, team capacity, and the comparable five-week data. It should then select one primary technical path for the next crop-cycle test, name its owner and resource envelope, set explicit success and stop criteria, and formally stop or defer the others. It should also decide whether existing field coverage is sustainable before adding deployments.

June 15 cannot establish 90-day field durability. Final rollout must wait for a representative full 90-day field cycle. A cell choice must also wait for certification scope, supply timing, economics, and field evidence; sleep mode requires acceptable latency and reliability evidence; solar requires performance across relevant light conditions. The sensors provide planning data rather than safety control, which lowers immediate safety exposure, but customer usefulness and continuity still require monitoring.
