# Decision memo

**To:** CanopyTrace leadership and board  
**Date:** May 11, 2026  
**Subject:** A five-week focus reset and a comparable 90-day battery decision

## Recommendation

Keep the 90-day no-visit objective, but do not declare a technical winner on the current evidence. For the next five weeks, make battery-cycle reliability the only active development priority, protect current-grower service, and pause the four unrelated roadmap efforts. Use this period to stop work churn, establish one comparable field-test protocol, learn whether battery visits matter to growers, and resolve the most important feasibility questions around each option. On June 15, leadership should choose which approach or approaches merit a representative 90-day field trial—not claim that the product objective has already been met.

This is a focus decision, not an emergency response. CanopyTrace has 22 months of runway, no active transaction, no safety or continuity incident, and support remains in its normal range. Those facts allow the company to trade five weeks of roadmap activity for better evidence and a sustainable pace.

## Act now

The CEO should use the existing written decision rights to name one accountable owner for the battery-cycle program and freeze staffing assignments through June 15. The CTO should own the common technical protocol and data quality; the product lead should own customer-impact learning; the named field-support owner should retain authority over service commitments. Escalations should follow the decision rights already in place rather than trigger informal engineer reassignment.

Create a common test protocol using the same sensor revision, reporting interval, battery thresholds, greenhouse-condition categories, and definitions of alert latency and battery-change failure. Record visits and unplanned checks as outcomes, not merely battery duration. Begin a limited comparative field run only where doing so will not impair contracted support. Five weeks cannot produce a completed crop-cycle result, but it can reveal setup flaws, early regressions, and whether a valid 90-day trial is ready to start.

Treat the three approaches as hypotheses with bounded work:

- For sleep mode, reproduce the profile on the common revision and measure battery draw and alert latency. A 36% bench projection and 18 field days are promising, but neither proves 90-day performance. The slowdown from 20 to 95 seconds is a real tradeoff. Although certified systems separately control irrigation and safety alarms, CanopyTrace still must determine whether slower planning alerts violate customer expectations or product commitments.
- For the higher-density cell, request a written certification scope, integration risks, landed-cost implications, minimum order constraints if any, and a realistic sample/procurement schedule. Do not redesign around it yet. The 108-day lab run is not representative field evidence, and the $18 cost, 12-week lead time, and unscoped certification may determine feasibility.
- For solar caps, standardize and instrument a small trial across sunny and shaded placements. Do not generalize from two sunny greenhouses. Four of ten shaded-row units reaching low battery before day 50 suggests that any viable use may be environment-specific.

In parallel, contact all 19 growers through a short, consistent interview or survey and review available account records. Ask how battery visits affect their labor, trust, renewal intent, expansion interest, and preferred tradeoffs, without implying an answer. The absence of crop loss or missed response windows shows that service has prevented recorded harm; it does not show that visits are costless or irrelevant.

## Do not do through June 15

Pause the cosmetic enclosure refresh, mobile analytics screen, small-farm kit, and reseller packaging. Preserve their current artifacts and decisions, but schedule no implementation, launch work, or “small” side tasks. Do not add new battery concepts, change test configurations midstream, promise growers a 90-day result, place a production cell order, begin an enclosure redesign for solar, or lower alert performance without an explicit acceptance criterion.

Do not use all three approaches being active as a reason to move people among them. Twenty-one reassignments in eight weeks already make results harder to compare and impose coordination costs. Also do not interpret the June 15 board date as a deadline to select a shipping solution; the board did not require one, and the necessary field evidence cannot exist by then.

## Sustainable operating plan

Reset plans to normal weekday capacity. No planned weekend trial checks and no continuation of 49-hour weeks. Any genuinely time-sensitive observation must be automated, deferred safely, or covered by a preassigned rotation with compensating weekday time. Reserve explicit weekly capacity for ordinary support and unexpected field issues before allocating trial work.

The field specialist should not remain the sole operational backstop. Cross-train one existing team member on visit triage, customer communications, and the documented response process, then publish primary and backup coverage through June 15. This does not change the named owner or dilute accountability. Current grower visits and contracted response windows take precedence over experimental checks; if capacity conflicts, reduce trial scope rather than extend hours.

Hold one short weekly program review based on a fixed dashboard: comparable device-days, battery thresholds, alert latency, visits, support performance, hours, and team pulse. Reassign people only for a customer-continuity issue or a decision by the authorized owner. The CEO should review workload weekly and remove scope when actual work exceeds normal capacity.

## June 15 decisions and later gate

On June 15, leadership should decide whether the 90-day objective remains the top company priority; which paused roadmap item, if any, may resume within normal capacity; and which battery approach or tightly justified pair advances into the representative trial. That downselection should use protocol-quality results, early field behavior, grower evidence, alert-latency acceptance, supplier timing and cost, and a scoped certification path. Leadership should also decide durable field-coverage staffing if the visit burden remains concentrated.

The final technical and product choice must wait for at least one comparable, representative 90-day field cycle. It should cover relevant greenhouse and placement conditions on the intended sensor revision and reporting interval, while measuring alert performance, battery-change visits, support load, failures, and customer response. Cell certification and supply feasibility must be established before committing that option. Economics should include device cost and service burden, but current records do not yet support assumptions about renewal, expansion, grower labor, or avoided visits. Until that gate is passed, every option remains provisional.
