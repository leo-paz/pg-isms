# Decision memo

**To:** CanopyTrace leadership and board  
**Date:** May 11, 2026  
**Subject:** Focus the next five weeks on credible battery evidence and sustainable execution

## Recommendation

CanopyTrace should make the 90-day battery objective the single active product-development priority through June 15. Pause active work on the cosmetic enclosure refresh, mobile analytics screen, small-farm kit, and reseller packaging. Preserve ordinary support for all 19 growers, continue collecting data from already-deployed trials when that can be done without extra weekend work, and use the five weeks to establish one comparable test program rather than declare a technical winner.

The company should not choose the higher-density cell merely because it reached 108 days in a lab, dismiss visits merely because no crop was lost, or keep seven initiatives active in the name of learning. Those conclusions are not supported by the evidence. The present trials differ in hardware revision and reporting interval, so their results are not directly comparable. None has completed a representative 90-day field cycle. Meanwhile, 21 engineer reassignments, 49-hour averages for four engineers, weekend checks, and a pulse decline from 7.3 to 5.0 show an operating problem now, even though customer response remains stable.

## Work through June 15

Name one accountable battery-program lead under the existing written decision rights. Give that person a stable, explicitly named team for five weeks; changes should require the relevant decision owner to remove an equal amount of work, not layer work on top. Engineers should return to their normal schedules, with no planned weekend trial checks. Trial monitoring should be scheduled during working hours. If a customer-support need conflicts with experimental work, current-grower service takes precedence and the experiment slips.

Before adding field units, the program lead should write a short common protocol covering:

- the current objective: no battery-change visit during a 90-day crop cycle;
- a common sensor revision, reporting interval, and data definition;
- conditions representative of the deployed fleet, identified from existing deployment records rather than guessed;
- measures for days to low-battery threshold and first change, visit count, missing data, alert-delivery latency, and other observed service effects;
- an unchanged baseline configuration for comparison; and
- predetermined review points and stopping conditions, including unacceptable alert delay or support burden.

The protocol should distinguish planning-data performance from irrigation and safety control. Separate certified systems reduce the consequence of slower sensor alerts, but they do not prove that a change from 20-second to 95-second median delivery is acceptable to growers or compatible with CanopyTrace's product promise. Product should therefore define and validate an alert-latency requirement with customers rather than invent one internally.

Use each approach according to what is actually known. Keep the sleep-mode profile in a bounded field evaluation because it has a 36% simulated improvement and an observed latency tradeoff that needs investigation. Do not roll it out fleet-wide. Preserve data from the existing solar units, including sunny and shaded rows, but do not expand solar deployment until the company understands why four of ten shaded units crossed the threshold before day 50 and whether the deployed fleet has conditions in which solar is suitable. For the higher-density cell, obtain a written cost and lead-time confirmation and scope certification work, schedule, and constraints; lab longevity alone does not justify procurement or customer deployment. These limited streams can be handled as one battery program with gates, not as three competing projects repeatedly borrowing engineers.

Product should contact the 19 paying growers during the five weeks, prioritizing those who requested visits while including some who did not. Ask consistently about grower labor, disruption, tolerance for visits and alert latency, and whether either affects renewal or expansion intent. Record answers separately from interpretation. This will not establish causal renewal impact in five weeks, but it will correct the present absence of customer-impact evidence. The product lead, not the field specialist, should own this work so field coverage is not further strained.

The named field-support owner should continue serving current growers. Leadership should designate and train backup coverage from available staff before expanding any trial that could generate visits. Until a backup is demonstrably ready, cap trial activity at what the current owner can support within normal hours after contracted service. No employee should be the automatic weekend backstop. Leadership should review workload, trial exceptions, customer service, and team pulse weekly in a short operating review; it should not use that meeting to reshuffle the team.

## What not to do before June 15

Do not restart the four paused roadmap efforts, begin a broad deployment of any battery approach, commit to a 12-week cell order, promise growers a 90-day result, or present projected or lab life as field proof. Do not change configurations mid-test without marking the result as a new cohort. Do not add weekend checks to protect an arbitrary calendar date. Finally, do not interpret the lack of crop loss or missed response windows as evidence that 94 visits were harmless; neither renewal, expansion, nor grower labor has been measured.

## June 15 decisions

Leadership should decide whether focus and staffing remain sustainable, which approach or deliberately defined combination advances to a representative 90-day field validation, what latency and support guardrails apply, and whether any paused roadmap item deserves re-entry. Re-entry should be limited: choose only work that can be staffed without disrupting the battery program or restoring overtime, and state what remains paused.

The June decision should be based on protocol-quality evidence gathered by then: comparable early field trends, the sleep-mode latency/customer requirement assessment, solar condition data, cell certification scope and economics, supplier lead time, customer interviews, support load, and team workload. If that evidence does not distinguish a candidate, leadership should continue the bounded comparison rather than force a winner for the board meeting.

Some decisions must wait beyond June 15. CanopyTrace should not claim the 90-day objective is met or authorize fleet-wide rollout until a candidate completes a representative 90-day field cycle under the common protocol. A cell commitment should also wait for certification scope and acceptable supply terms; a solar-wide decision should wait for evidence across relevant light conditions; and any claim about renewal or expansion impact should wait for actual behavioral evidence, not interviews alone. The company's 22 months of runway and absence of an active financing, acquisition, safety, or continuity crisis allow this disciplined delay, but do not remove the need to protect service and team capacity now.
