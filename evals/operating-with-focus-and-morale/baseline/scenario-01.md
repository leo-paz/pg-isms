# Four-week operating plan

The company’s immediate constraint is not credibility; it is reliable service and founder attention. For the next four weeks, the CEO should make customer trust and product execution the default use of her time while preserving only obligations that are legally, financially, or operationally essential.

## Today: reset priorities and the calendar

The CEO should tell the team plainly that her calendar has pulled her away from the company’s highest-risk work, apologize for the repeated retailer rescheduling, and name two company priorities for the month:

1. Resolve and verify the data-sync defect affecting nine customers.
2. Reestablish a dependable cadence with customers and the team.

Pause the reporting redesign until the defect is resolved and stable. Assign one technical owner to the defect, one person to customer communication, and define the CEO’s role: remove blockers, make tradeoffs quickly, and speak directly with affected retailers. Existing decision rights remain in place; this is a priority reset, not centralization around the founder.

Cancel or defer conference planning, optional accelerator events, podcasts, and networking coffees during the four weeks. Decline new external commitments. Keep only obligations whose cancellation would create a concrete near-term cost: required accelerator sessions, signed contractual appearances, critical partner meetings, board or regulatory duties, and genuinely time-sensitive financing matters. Since there is no active fundraise and fourteen months of runway, routine investor coffees are not essential. If a commitment can be handled by another teammate, shortened, recorded asynchronously, or moved beyond the month, do that.

The CEO should personally contact the six rescheduled retailers today, acknowledge the inconvenience without making excuses, and offer fixed meeting times within five business days. The nine affected customers should receive a separate update stating what is known, who owns the issue, the next update time, and any available workaround. Do not promise a repair date until engineering has scoped the defect.

## Week 1: contain and diagnose

Create a small defect response group and give it priority over planned feature work. Reproduce the failure, identify affected data and customers, establish a rollback or workaround if possible, and define acceptance tests for the fix. Hold a 15-minute daily incident check-in limited to decisions, blockers, customer impact, and the next communication. Avoid broad status meetings; use a written update for everyone else.

The CEO should complete the six retailer calls and join calls with the nine affected customers where the relationship is at risk. She should ask what operational harm the defect caused, what recovery they need, and what would restore confidence. Capture findings in one shared customer-impact list rather than creating new projects from every request.

By the end of the week, the team should have a root-cause hypothesis, a tested containment plan, a named release decision-maker, and an honest customer update. If the defect is larger than expected, explicitly move the reporting redesign and other lower-priority work rather than asking the team to do both.

## Week 2: fix and communicate

Implement and review the smallest safe fix, test it against the failure modes and affected customer data, and stage the rollout with monitoring and a rollback plan. The CEO should protect the engineers from unrelated requests and make any scope or resource decisions the same day.

Send affected retailers scheduled updates even if there is no breakthrough. Once the fix is validated, explain what changed, what customers need to do, and how the company will verify recovery. Offer reasonable remediation where the defect caused material disruption, but decide it consistently rather than improvising on individual calls.

At the end of Week 2, review three facts: whether all affected accounts are identified, whether the fix has passed agreed tests, and whether each customer has a recovery owner. Do not declare the incident complete merely because code shipped.

## Week 3: verify recovery and learn

Monitor sync success, data correctness, support volume, and customer confirmation. Close the incident only when the technical metrics are stable and affected retailers confirm that normal operations have resumed. Conduct a short, blame-free review covering cause, detection gaps, response delays, and safeguards. Turn the result into a small number of owned actions with dates—for example, sync monitoring, alerting, regression coverage, and an incident communication template.

The CEO should hold a brief one-on-one with each team member. The purpose is to hear where her external schedule or shifting attention created delays, clarify what support they need, and reinforce existing decision rights. She should not use these conversations to defend the prior calendar.

Only after customer recovery is verified should the team reassess the reporting redesign. Decide whether to resume, reduce, or stop it based on retailer value and current capacity; do not automatically preserve sunk work.

## Week 4: install a sustainable cadence

Review the month with the team and a small set of measures: defect status and recurrence, affected-customer recovery, completed retailer conversations, unresolved customer commitments, product progress, and founder time allocation. Use the review to set the next six weeks’ top one or two outcomes.

Adopt calendar guardrails for the CEO:

- Reserve at least three days a week for customers, product decisions, hiring or coaching, and uninterrupted company work.
- Cap external credibility-building activity at four hours a week unless tied to a specific current company objective.
- Hold one weekly leadership review and replace routine internal status meetings with written updates.
- Require every new commitment to identify the outcome, owner, time cost, and what it displaces.
- Review the next two weeks of the calendar every Friday with an operations owner or trusted teammate.

External visibility is useful only when it serves a defined need such as recruiting, a key partnership, customer acquisition, or financing. Track those outcomes rather than attendance or exposure. The CEO can resume selected external activity after the four-week reset, but only within the cap and only when the customer and product commitments for the week are protected.

The key behavioral standard is reliability: fewer promises, kept consistently. This plan preserves essential obligations while making clear that optional commitments are not equal to customer harm, product reliability, or the team’s ability to execute.
