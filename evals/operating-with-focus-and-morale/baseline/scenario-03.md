# Operating plan for the next month

The company should stop treating the conference as an emergency. The evidence says the current pace is already reducing output: decisions are being reversed after implementation, production changes are failing, customer conversations are being missed, and engineering is blocked on late-night approvals. With sixteen months of runway, no fundraise, and no payroll threat, another four-week push is more likely to damage the product and team than prove seriousness.

## 1. Reset the conference goal within 24 hours

Define the smallest credible conference outcome: one reliable story, one stable demo, and a clear way to capture qualified leads. The goal is not to finish every planned feature.

- Freeze the conference scope immediately. Keep only work required for the core demo, lead capture, and essential customer-facing materials.
- Prefer a controlled demo environment, recorded backup, or existing stable product flow over a risky production release.
- Cancel or defer every item that does not materially improve the conference conversation. Publish the cut list so work does not quietly return.
- Name one conference owner who is not the CEO. That person runs the checklist, coordinates deadlines, and escalates only decisions that meet explicit criteria.
- Set a code cutoff at least five business days before the event. After that, allow only critical fixes reviewed by two engineers. Rehearse the demo twice and prepare a fallback if connectivity or the product fails.

If the reduced scope still cannot be delivered during normal working hours, reduce it again. A smaller dependable showing is better than an ambitious unstable one.

## 2. Remove the CEO as an approval bottleneck

The CEO should take at least two full days away from work now, then operate on a capped schedule for the remainder of the month: no routine night work, one protected day off each week, and roughly 45–50 working hours at most. This is a risk-control measure, not a reward. Her recent decision quality and availability indicate that additional hours are counterproductive.

For the next four weeks:

- Give each engineering lead written authority over releases in a defined area, including the authority to say no to late changes.
- Establish a daytime decision window. Questions raised after it proceed under the delegated rule or wait until the next day; engineers should not wait for late-night approval.
- Require material product reversals to include a short written rationale, impact assessment, and agreement from the responsible lead before implementation changes.
- Route only irreversible, company-level decisions to the CEO. Product details, release sequencing, and incident responses belong to named owners.
- Ask a board member, adviser, or trusted peer to check in with the CEO twice during the month and hold the workload boundary.

The CEO should spend her limited conference time on the work only she can do: key customer conversations, positioning, selected partnerships, and team alignment.

## 3. Stabilize operations before adding work

For the first week, pause nonessential production releases. Review the three rollbacks and three reversed release decisions in one blameless session. Identify whether the recurring causes are unclear ownership, inadequate testing, rushed review, or changing requirements, then assign one corrective action and owner for each pattern.

Restore customer contact immediately:

- Reschedule the nine missed calls within five business days, with a direct apology and no elaborate excuse.
- Assign a backup attendee to every scheduled customer call.
- Have the CEO attend only the highest-value calls; distribute the rest across product, engineering, and customer-facing staff.
- Summarize recurring customer needs weekly so conference messaging and the product roadmap reflect evidence rather than urgency.

Use a simple release gate for the month: named owner, acceptance criteria, test evidence, rollback plan, and daytime monitoring. No release should depend on an exhausted executive making an ad hoc decision at night.

## 4. Run the month in weekly stages

**Week 1:** Cut conference scope, delegate authority, pause risky releases, reschedule customer calls, and give the CEO recovery time.

**Week 2:** Finish only the minimum conference package, complete operational fixes from the rollback review, and rehearse the demo.

**Week 3:** Hold the code cutoff, run final rehearsals, confirm lead-routing and follow-up ownership, and protect normal sleep and days off.

**Week 4:** Execute the event with the stable package. Follow up with promising leads within 48 hours, but do not launch a post-conference sprint.

At the end of each week, review five measures: CEO hours, decisions awaiting CEO approval, production rollbacks, completed customer calls, and conference-critical items remaining. The board or leadership team should intervene if CEO hours rise again, approvals accumulate overnight, or stability worsens.

Success after one month means the company presented a reliable product story, kept production stable, restored customer conversations, and proved it can operate without heroic hours. That demonstrates seriousness more convincingly than a brittle last-minute feature push.
