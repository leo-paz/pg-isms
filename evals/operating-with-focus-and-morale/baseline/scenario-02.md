# 30-day operating plan

## What to persist with

Persist with the mission and the narrow reminder use case. The daily-use clinic’s 18% reduction in no-shows is the strongest evidence available: the product can create meaningful value when it becomes part of normal work. Keep serving that clinic well, verify that the result is real and repeatable, and learn exactly which users, actions, and conditions produced it.

Do not interpret the competitor announcement, two engineers’ reactions, or the product lead’s confidence as market evidence. The relevant evidence is customer behavior: one successful daily user, one blocked deployment, one poorly adopted workflow, and two paid pilots from fourteen demos. That is mixed and incomplete—not proof of either failure or product-market fit.

## What to change

Stop executing the current plan unchanged. The priority is not more features or more hours; it is resolving the two visible barriers to repeatable adoption:

1. **Workflow friction.** Double entry is an explicit reason alerts are ignored. Spend the month simplifying the workflow, ideally by integrating with the clinic’s existing process or, if that is too large for 30 days, by removing the highest-friction duplicate steps.
2. **Implementation and procurement.** A signature without launch is not a successful customer. Create a lightweight launch playbook with a named clinic owner, procurement checklist, data requirements, training date, and target go-live date.
3. **Customer qualification.** Compare the successful clinic with the two stalled clinics. Focus sales on clinics that resemble the daily user in urgency, workflow, authority, and technical readiness. Pause broad selling until the team can state who adopts quickly and why.
4. **Measurement.** Track activation and sustained use before celebrating contracts. Core measures should be time from signature to launch, percentage of intended staff active weekly, alert-open rate, workflow completion rate, and change in no-shows.

## How to operate for 30 days

### Days 1–5: align and diagnose

- Tell the team the month has one objective: determine whether the proven outcome can be reproduced with an adoptable workflow.
- Assign three accountable owners: product/engineering for workflow adoption, customer success for launches, and sales/founder for customer qualification. Do not create parallel feature projects.
- Interview users at all three clinics, including frontline staff rather than only buyers. Observe the successful clinic’s workflow and the third clinic’s double-entry process directly.
- Establish baseline metrics for each clinic and document the successful clinic’s no-show calculation so the 18% result can be trusted.
- Ask the stalled clinic for a concrete procurement map and decision date. If no path or owner exists, classify it as blocked rather than active.

### Days 6–15: remove the largest obstacle

- Choose the smallest product or operational change likely to eliminate most double entry. Define the expected behavioral result before building it—for example, increasing alert opens from 22% to at least 50% among trained staff.
- Prototype with staff from the low-adoption clinic, test quickly, and release only after they can complete the workflow with materially less effort.
- Turn the successful clinic’s setup into a repeatable onboarding checklist.
- Continue essential reliability and support work, but freeze unrelated features.

### Days 16–25: run focused adoption tests

- Relaunch the improved workflow at the low-adoption clinic with a named champion, brief training, and daily usage monitoring.
- Try to launch the procurement-stalled clinic only if the clinic supplies an owner, dated steps, and a plausible go-live within the month.
- Use several targeted sales conversations to test the emerging ideal-customer profile. The goal is learning and qualified commitments, not maximizing demo count.
- Hold short twice-weekly reviews of facts: usage, obstacles, experiments, and next actions. Change tactics when evidence changes; do not change the month’s objective impulsively.

### Days 26–30: decide from evidence

At month-end, make an explicit continue, adjust, or stop decision. Continue investing in this workflow if the team can show sustained usage at the first clinic, a material adoption improvement at the third, and at least one credible path to repeat deployment. Adjust the target segment or delivery model if outcomes remain strong only under specific conditions. Consider a larger pivot if clinics still will not launch or use the product after the main friction is removed.

## Team norms

No mandatory nights. Exhaustion will reduce judgment and turn uncertainty into conflict. Set a normal workweek, protect one or two priorities per person, and make tradeoffs visible when new work appears. Give the engineers’ concern a fair hearing, but require claims from both skeptics and advocates to be tied to customer evidence. The product lead should own prioritization, not demand limitless effort.

End each week by sharing what was learned, what changed, and what will not be pursued. Recognize customer-facing learning and obstacle removal, not hours worked. The team should finish the month with a clearer market thesis, a less burdensome product, and enough energy to act on the result.
