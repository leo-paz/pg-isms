# Six-week operating plan

The bottleneck is not mainly the CEO's calendar. It is an operating system in which too many decisions have one approver, while commitments and incident authority are not reliably communicated. The goal for six weeks is to move routine authority to named leaders, make the remaining CEO decisions fast and visible, and preserve runway by preventing unpriced work and uncontrolled commitments.

## Outcomes by the end of week six

- No decision waits more than two business days without an explicit escalation.
- Every pricing exception, enterprise promise, incident, roadmap change, and senior hire has one accountable decision owner.
- No customer commitment reaches delivery teams after signature.
- The CEO personally decides only a short, written set of high-consequence exceptions.
- Leads can explain their authority, escalation triggers, and current priorities without referring everything upward.

## Week 1: Stabilize and expose the queues

The CEO appoints one temporary operating lead, likely the COO or strongest cross-functional department lead, to run this six-week change. Create a single decision register containing the 18 delayed decisions and all new decisions. Each entry has an owner, decision needed, deadline, affected people, and escalation path. Clear the existing backlog in two time-boxed sessions, deciding, delegating, or rejecting every item.

Immediately establish incident command: the first qualified responder names one incident commander, who owns decisions until an explicit handoff. Technical and communications leads advise but do not create shared authority. Run a short review of the two recent incidents and publish the resulting protocol.

Freeze new nonstandard enterprise promises until the approval and handoff rules below are active. This is a control measure, not a permanent sales slowdown.

## Week 2: Write decision rights and commitment gates

In a working session with department leads, define one accountable owner and escalation thresholds for the five recurring decision classes:

- Pricing exceptions: Sales or Revenue leadership may approve within written discount, term, margin, and liability bands. Only exceptions beyond those bands reach the CEO.
- Enterprise delivery promises: a named commercial owner can offer only pre-approved capabilities and dates. Any custom scope or accelerated date requires written Product and Engineering feasibility approval before signature.
- Incident priorities: the incident commander decides during the event; the CEO is consulted only for predefined business, legal, or existential thresholds.
- Roadmap changes: the Product lead owns sequencing within the agreed company priorities and capacity envelope. The CEO decides only changes that materially alter strategy, runway, or a board-level commitment.
- Senior hiring: functional leaders own the process and recommendation within an approved headcount and compensation plan. The CEO retains approval only for direct reports or roles above an explicit level or compensation threshold.

Publish this as a one-page decision-rights table: decision, owner, input required, guardrails, escalation trigger, and response deadline. Every delegated owner must have real authority to say yes or no.

Also install a commitment gate in the CRM or contract workflow: no nonstandard delivery language can be signed without named Product and Engineering approval, and every signed commitment automatically produces an owner, delivery record, and notification to affected teams within one business day.

## Weeks 3–4: Operate the new system

Run a 20-minute twice-weekly decision review led by the operating lead. It covers only overdue decisions, cross-functional conflicts, and proposed changes to guardrails. Owners resolve ordinary work outside the meeting. Use a two-business-day service level for CEO escalations; if she misses it, either the designated delegate decides or the default written rule applies.

Department leads review the decision table with their teams and practice three recent examples, including one where they decide independently and one that requires escalation. The CEO should visibly support reasonable decisions she might have made differently. Reversals should occur only when guardrails were breached or important new evidence appears; otherwise delegation will remain nominal.

At the end of week four, inspect the register, contract handoffs, and incident records. Tighten ambiguous thresholds and remove approvals that are still traveling through the CEO without a stated reason.

## Weeks 5–6: Measure, correct, and institutionalize

Track a small weekly scorecard: number and age of waiting decisions, percentage decided by the designated owner, number of post-signature surprise commitments, percentage of incidents with an incident commander named promptly, CEO escalations by category, and reversals of delegated decisions. Include short qualitative feedback from leads on where authority remains unclear.

In week five, address recurring failure patterns: narrow guardrails where risk is uncontrolled, broaden them where safe decisions still escalate, and coach owners who avoid making calls. In week six, make the decision-rights table permanent, assign an owner to review it monthly, and reduce the decision review to the lightest cadence supported by the data. The CEO and leads should agree on the next six-week company priorities so delegated decisions share a common direction.

## Where the personal focus plan belongs

Calendar pruning, a productivity system, and two protected mornings may help the CEO execute her genuinely nondelegable work, but they are a supporting action in week two, after decision rights are defined. Her protected time should be used for strategy, key talent, and the few exceptions that cross written thresholds. She should keep one predictable escalation window and a backup delegate rather than becoming unreachable. Personal focus can increase the throughput of one person; it cannot fix unclear authority, late commitments, or ownerless incidents. The six-week test is therefore whether the company makes sound decisions without routinely waiting for the CEO, not whether her calendar looks cleaner.
